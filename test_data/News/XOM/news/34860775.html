        ENERGY COULD BE LEFT OUT:Energy provisions of all stripes are likely to be left out of negotiations over the latest, and possibly last, pandemic relief bill this year before the election.

        As Congress returns after a long July 4 layoff, the main items on the table are reportedly additional payments to individuals, money for state and local governments, extended unemployment insurance, and liability protection for companies.

        Congress has already approved $3 trillion in pandemic relief aid, including $1.7 trillion doled out to a range of affected companies, a list that has included oil and gas and renewable firms, and the latest bill could include a few trillion more.

        But the needs of the energy industry seem less top-of-mind for policymakers today. The oil market has somewhat recovered from its lows, with prices stabilizing at around $40 per barrel, and that’s after Democrats rejected help for the industry during the worst of its woes.

        Wind and solar energy groups have lobbied Congress for help, with Democrats interested but not prioritizing their needs, as companies faced supply chain disruptions, travel restrictions, and other challenges from the pandemic that delayed projects.

        The Treasury Department has already moved to give the renewable industry part of what it wants, extending by one year “safe harbor” deadlines for wind and solar tax credits to account for disruptions from the pandemic.

        Meanwhile the congressional approach to climate change seems as partisan as ever, as Democrats just got through weeks of releasing big plans, while Republicans continue to pan the efforts as Green New Deal-esque.

        CHEVRON MOVES FIRST WITH BIGGEST OIL DEAL SINCE PANDEMIC:U.S. oil and gas giant Chevron announced Monday it is buying independent producer Noble Energy in the first major — and undoubtedly not the last — corporate acquisition resulting from the pandemic-fueled price collapse.

        The $5 billion all-stock transaction increases Chevron’s footprint in the Permian Basin.

        Analysts expect more bankruptcies and consolidation in the oil patch prompted by the market crash, as big multinational companies able to head off the fiscal challenges pounce on the struggles of smaller operators.

        “Our strong balance sheet and financial discipline gives us the flexibility to be a buyer of quality assets during these challenging times,” said Chevron Chairman and CEO Michael Wirth.

        It also strengthens Chevron’s position in the Eastern Mediterranean, including in Israel, where Noble operates Leviathan, a massive offshore natural gas project.

        “Noble’s position in Israel is the company’s crown jewel,” said Jean-Baptiste Bouzard of research group WoodMacKenzie. “Israel will provide Chevron with a new core international geography that will rebalance the portfolio towards gas and provide a springboard to capture further upside potential in the region.”

        If you remember: Chevron made an unsuccessful and (much larger) $50 billion bid to purchase rival Anadarko Petroleum last year, losing out to Occidental Petroleum.

        RETURN TO NORMAL DEMAND IS A FAR WAY OFF, ESPECIALLY FOR JET FUEL:U.S. consumption of oil and oil-based products won’t return to normal until August 2021, the Energy Information Administration said in note Monday.

        Demand for petroleum products has increased as states relaxed stay-at-home restrictions, but levels will remain below 2019 levels for several more months, EIA projected.

        Flying will be the activity slowest to comeback, with EIA expecting jet fuel demand to be 31% lower than the 2019 average for the rest of this year, a much larger percentage reduction projected in 2020 for gasoline and distillate (both down 10%).

        EIA expects jet fuel consumption to average 1.5 million barrels per day in 2021, or about 12% lower than its 2019 average. By contrast, EIA assumes as employment levels return to normal in 2021, gasoline consumption will increase to 9.1 million barrels per day, or to about 2% less than its 2019 average.

        JOBS IN PLUGGING ABANDONED OIL AND GAS WELLS:Congress could create as many as 120,000 jobs if it created a federal program to fund the plugging of abandoned oil wells, a long-standing environmental hazard that is growing worse because of the price crash, according to a new report Monday.

        Such a program could be a boon for oil and gas workers, say analysts from Columbia University’s Center on Global Energy Policy, who note the industry has seen employment drop to its lowest level since 2006, “but appears able to scale up to carry out this work.”

        Joe Biden, in his revamped climate plan last week, pledged to create 250,000 jobs with a federal program to plug orphaned and abandoned oil and gas wells.

        There are anywhere from several hundred thousand to 3 million orphaned and abandoned wells across the U.S., which can leak methane and other pollutants that can contaminate air and water. While states and the federal government require oil and gas companies to post bonds or other forms of financial assurance to pay for well plugging in case producers go bankrupt, the payments are insufficient to cover the cost.

        If Congress were to create a program plugging the 56,600 orphaned wells that have been documented by the Interstate Oil and Gas Compact Commission, it could cost $1.4 billion to $2.7 billion, according to the Columbia paper. Expanding the program to identify and plug 500,000 wells to include those unaccounted for could cost between $12 and $24 billion.

        A program like this would reduce local air pollution, safety risks, and greenhouse gas emissions at a cost of $67 to $170 per ton of CO2-equivalent, the paper says, “well within the range of other policy options.”

        ENERGY DEPARTMENT ANNOUNCES NEW HYDROGEN INVESTMENTS:The Energy Department announced more than $60 million in new investments today for hydrogen applications it believes can provide “more high value-added use” in the next five to 10 years, said Michael Berube, acting deputy assistant secretary for transportation in the department’s renewables energy office.

        Those applications include data centers, marine use, ports, and steel production, Berube said during a virtual event hosted Monday by the Atlantic Council. It builds on a $100 million hydrogen initiative the department announced a few weeks ago.

        Two-pronged hydrogen strategy: Berube said the U.S. is focusing on two main areas for hydrogen production and use. The first is the transportation sector, where the department sees a role for hydrogen fuel cells to reduce emissions from heavy-duty trucking, shipping, and maybe even rail, he said.

        The Energy Department is also exploring hydrogen’s role as an “integrating energy source,” and it sees potential for hydrogen to help balance an electricity grid with increasing renewables and to decarbonize harder-to-reach industrial sectors like steel production, Berube said.

        BROUILLETTE SIGNS DEAL WITH INDIA ON OIL RESERVES:Energy Secretary Dan Brouillette said Friday he signed an agreement to begin cooperating with India on operating Strategic Petroleum Reserves, including the possibility of India storing crude in the U.S.’ emergency stockpile.

        Brouillette told reporters in a conference call with India’s Minister of Petroleum and Natural Gas Dharmendra Pradhan that they would iron out the details of the collaboration in coming months. He said it could look like a similar program DOE established recently with Australia, which in April committed to building an SPR while storing some crude in the U.S.’ stockpile.

        “There is no predetermined outcome,” Brouillette said. “We are excited to begin the conversation with India. It’s important for both of our nations.”

        CONSERVATIVE GROUPS TO LOBBY CONGRESS ON CLEAN ENERGY THIS WEEK:Citizens for Responsible Energy Solutions Forum and the Conservative Energy Network are holding a three-day virtual “fly-in” beginning Monday encouraging Republicans in Congress to invest in clean energy as part of economic recovery efforts.

        The groups have organized more than 90 business leaders, farmers, state officials and others from around the country to participate in 70 meetings with GOP lawmakers.

        “Conservatives recognize the value of clean energy to the economy, national security and public health, and as part of an all-of-the-above energy mix,” said Heather Reams, executive director of CRES Forum.

        CRES also sent a letter last week to Republican leaders asking them to prioritize passing the America’s Transportation Infrastructure Act, a highway bill that spends on EV charging stations, and the Lisa Murkowski/Joe Manchin-led American Energy Innovation Act this Congress.

        JUDGE TOSSES TRUMP CHALLENGE TO CALIFORNIA CAP-AND-TRADE:California’s cap-and-trade system doesn’t conflict with U.S. foreign policy or violate the Constitution, a federal district court judge in California ruled Friday, handing a legal victory to the state on its signature climate program.

        “Overall, the United States has failed to identify a clear and express foreign policy that directly conflicts with California’s cap-and-trade program,” Judge William Shubb said in his order. The Trump administration had argued the California program’s link with Quebec was unlawful, because it conflicted with the federal government’s authority over foreign policy, including President Trump’s withdrawal from the Paris climate agreement.

        Shubb, however, said in his ruling that the Justice Department offered “no concrete evidence” that California’s program “interfered with either negotiations for a better deal or the nation’s imminent withdrawal” from the global climate deal.

        EXXON TEES UP ANOTHER CLIMATE CASE VENUE FIGHT:ExxonMobil filed Friday moving a climate case brought by D.C. Attorney General Karl Racine from D.C.’s Superior Court to a federal district court, teeing up yet another battle over where such lawsuits should be heard.

        D.C.’s case, filed in late June, seeks to bust Exxon, Shell, BP, and Chevron for misleading the public about climate change science and the role their products plan in increasing carbon emissions. Exxon, though, argues the lawsuit is political in nature and has national policy implications, which is why it should be heard by a federal court.

        Oil companies’ record in venue fights isn’t great: Exxon has already lost three other bids to move climate change cases brought by cities and counties to federal court. The oil majors and its allies, however, are hoping the Supreme Court will eventually weigh in on where these cases should be heard. Catch up on the legal landscape in Abby’s recent story.
