“We Don’t Always Have Certainty”: Inside the Rise and Partial Fall of a COVID-19 Wonder Drug Stock

A promising report about Gilead’s remdesivir moved the whole market, generating massive profits that raised calls for an SEC review. Later reports painted a much less positive picture.
ByWilliam D. Cohan
April 27, 2020

Donald Trump isn’t a doctor, but he’s been playing one on TV.

First came Spravato, an antidepressant drug made by a subsidiary of Johnson & Johnson that, as I reported in February, Trump pushed on veterans to help them try to fight their PTSD and suicidal thoughts. The controversial drug apparently doesn’t really help veterans as much as hoped, but Trump’s advocacy of it did help his friends, among them Woody Johnson, the U.S. Ambassador to the U.K. (and J&J heir), and Alex Gorsky, the CEO of Johnson & Johnson. Then came Trump’s relentless campaign for the antimalarial drug hydroxychloroquine as a potential cure for the effects of the coronavirus. That has also been widely discredited, and he hasn’t mentioned it in a few days. And then, of course, there is Trump’s truly bizarre references to injecting disinfectant—Clorox, Lysol, anyone?—into our bodies as a way to kill the virus. This too has been totally debunked as downright dangerous; Trump has since said he was being “sarcastic,” although he has a strange sense of sarcasm, if true.

But there’s one drug—remdesivir, made by Gilead Sciences, a $100-billion-market-cap company—that people are hoping will make a difference in the battle against the virus and about which Trump has been mostly silent. (In March, he called remdesivir “promising” and has said little of it since.) The remdesivir rocket lifted off the launch pad on April 16, after the stock market closed, when Stat, a little-known Boston–based, health care-oriented media organization founded by Rick Berke, a former longtime New York Times reporter and Politico executive editor, broke the news that a Chicago hospital, treating severe COVID-19 patients with remdesivir, found that their fevers and respiratory symptoms diminished rapidly with the use of the Gilead drug. University of Chicago Medicine recruited 125 patients who volunteered for the study. Of the 125, 113 had severe COVID. “The best news is that most of our patients have already been discharged, which is great,” Kathleen Mullane, the University of Chicago infectious disease specialist overseeing the remdesivir studies for the hospital, said in a video recording to her colleagues that Stat obtained. “We’ve only had two patients perish.” The two Stat reporters made clear in their reporting that “[t]he outcomes offer only a snapshot of remdesivir’s effectiveness.”

The news had an immediate impact. Traders at the Chicago Mercantile Exchange started snapping up E-minis, option contracts tied to the S&P 500. They were betting the Stat report about the effectiveness of remdesivir would jump-start the stock market, driving prices up on Friday, April 17. According to a trader who trades E-minis, within minutes of the Stat report, one trader or a group of traders bought around 100,000 S&P E-minis. The S&P Index then shot up about 50 points in after-hours trading, giving the trader or traders a cool profit, on paper anyway, of $250 million. “I didn’t know what triggered the initial moon shot on April 16th just after the close,” the startled trader emailed me on April 16. “I just saw the JUMP in volume and price that made no sense. Then I said I’d like to see what news sparked this rally. And we found out later that it was the Gilead story.”

Gilead stock also took off. Right after the Stat report was published it hit $76.40, then $83, and by 5:40 p.m., it was trading at more than $89 per share, the trader observed. The fierce trading in Gilead caught the attention of Lloyd Doggett, a Democratic congressman from Texas and the chairman of the House Ways and Means Health Subcommittee. Doggett asked the Securities and Exchange Commission to do a “thorough” investigation of the trading in Gilead shares around the time of the Stat report to make sure there was no insider trading involved. “Providing information that’s designed to impact the stock market is not something that is permitted under federal securities law,” Doggett told CNN. On April 17, the Dow Jones Industrial Average was up more than 700 points on the remdesivir news, or nearly 3%. The markets were giddy with the prospect that remdesivir might work. People were too.

But a week later came the sobering news that, in a Chinese trial, the drug “flopped,” according to a report in the Financial Times. The researchers studied 237 patients, of which 158 got the drug and remaining 79 patients did not. Citing draft documents published accidentally by the World Health Organization and seen by the FT, the newspaper said remdesivir “did not improve patients’ condition or reduce the pathogen’s presence in the bloodstream.” Gilead disputed the findings of the Chinese study. An April 23 Stat story about the Chinese study included the headline that Gilead “still sees reason for hope.” But, once again, traders didn’t wait around. Trading in Gilead’s stock was halted and then fell by as much as 9% to about $74 a share. (It’s now around $80 per share.)

In the absence of further word from Donald Trump, M.D. about the efficacy of remdesivir, I contacted Berke, the Stat cofounder, to see if he had any insight into whether Gilead’s antiviral drug works, or not. I also asked him whether it was standard practice to publish stories about a clinical trial underway before the full results had been established, as happened with Stat’s first story about the University of Chicago hospital that put markets in a tizzy. He was a little cagey, initially. Understandably, he wondered what my “angle” was for the story, given the remdesivir roller coaster. Finally, he agreed to answer my question about how much faith we should be putting in the drug and then how a publication reports on ongoing clinical trials.

“You and everyone else is eager to know whether remdesivir works,” he wrote in an email. “If we had the answer you can be sure we would have published that story—and trumpeted it far and wide. We are fortunate at STAT to have several reporters who have authoritatively and painstakingly covered clinical trial results for decades and know this world inside out. But as both stories you cite make clear, the process of understanding whether a new drug works can involve lots of contradictory information. As both stories note, right down to the headline of the first, which uses the word ‘suggests,’ we don’t always have certainty. Regarding your question about our original Gilead story, we and other journalism organizations report on the results of clinical trials in various phases all the time. It is not the preferred practice for health journalists to wait on reporting on results until there is a clear, final answer. Quite the contrary, we help surface information that can lead to that final answer.”
