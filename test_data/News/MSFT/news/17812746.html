Talk that sport will change forever is right on the money

Peter FitzSimons
Columnist and author
April 3, 2020 — 7.45pm
April 3, 2020 — 7.45pm
, register or subscribe to save articles for later.

Save articles for later

Add articles to your saved list and come back to them any time.

It is the question of among sports people around the world. What will our games look like, after the plague?

And the answer ...?

No-one has a clue. In this fast-changing environment where something like the NBA could be shut down in two hours on the basis of one positive test, it is preposterous to predict what things will look like six months or a year from now.

For my money the only certainty is ... less money.

The one universal theme through all the sports is vicious cut-backs for players, mass redundancies for staff and shocking loss of revenue flow that they had been counting on – with the shortfall being made up mostly by loans. For those who survive financially to the other side, there will be a determination never to live so high off the hog again and so things like the NRL spending half a million dollars a day every day, just to run a comp, simply won't come again. Ditto Rugby Australia with over 100 employees when it was run by three people in the days before professionalism.

The old Microsoft line I remember was the "shred-a-head", policy, meaning that if it could not be demonstrated that any particular employee actively added to the bottom line, then they were to be shown the door. Expect that in professional sport. Both head office and support staff will shrink markedly.

And sport can also expect that, just like this nation, the need to pay back the money loaned to get us through the next six months or a year will have to be paid back. Not only will the sports have to pay that back, but also the sponsors and fans on which sport lives. Think Qantas – if they do renew rugby sponsorship, we can expect it to be for a much smaller figure.

Advertisement

All up, the pool of money from which the players draw will be commensurately a lot smaller. The days of Valentine Holmes and the like getting million-dollar contracts are gone, just as rugby paying similar money for David Pocock to have a sabbatical year.

When we resume after this rude interruption, sport will be smaller, more stream-lined, less bloated on its own success – while perhaps also being more treasured than ever because so many of us realise now, how much we miss it now that its gone.

Too close for comfort

Still, with every other sport shut down, remind me please why horse racing is still going? A reader, Trevor Barlin, sent an image of the start of last week's Golden Slipper. It shows all sixteen of the horses loaded in the starting stalls – measuring 17.2 metres long by 2.8 metres wide – with sixteen jockeys and at least seven barrier attendants. Even before the Stage 2 restrictions, the government regulations on safe distance in confined spaces was four square metres per person.

In this case with 48 square metres for 23 people, it is just over half that. Why? Why does every other sport, rightly, get shut down, but racing is exempt. At least in Tassie they have shut it down, while in WA the calls are getting stronger.

Sprinting's just not our thing

Yonks back, you might recall, TFF did a careful piece positing a theory as to why Jamaica had produced more world-record-breaking runners than any other country on earth. In essence, the theory held that because Jamaica was the most distant Caribbean nation from Africa when the wretched slave trade was at its height, it meant that only the strongest slaves could withstand the journey, and their gene pool started bubbling and fizzing with strength and athleticism because of it.

This week on Twitter, a bloke by the name of Tim Wheeler gave a corollary theory as to why Australia has – with notable exceptions like Cathy Freeman and Nova Peris – generally struggled in athletics.

"We've never been the powerhouse in world athletics like we are in swimming," he notes. "The reason is we are descended from a bunch of woeful runners that couldn't escape from Constable Plod with a loaf of bread under an arm."

Maybe the broad mass of the Australian population should have tried baseball, where we could at least have been genetically disposed towards brilliance, in stealing bases?

Touch is out, but connection isn't

And of course it's not just elite sport we are missing. Many of us are also missing our amateur sporting comps. Personally, I miss my basketball team the Lizards, my mates at touch footy. For many it is such interaction that is key to ongoing good mental health and its absence puts that health in danger.

Take the "Take it Easy Eagles" touch footy team of the Hornsby Touch Association for example.

"We are not great as touch footy players," their stalwart Matt Macoustra writes to me, "more like poetry in slow motion, but we are great mates there to support each other through thick and thin. Our wives and partners have long ago stopped asking why it takes three hours to play a 50-minute game of footy… those sideline talks that stretch into Wednesday evening are a great support to us all."

But now no more of that? Like hell.

This Wednesday night their skipper, David Millar will have them all online, from their lounge rooms or back decks, doing what they always do – talking shit. Best tries! Better lies! Their lives, how they and their families are travelling through this whole thing.

"I have been blessed to have had a lifelong connection with sport," he writes. "The benefits come not from making a red cent from playing the game, but from the friendship, support and sense of belonging to 'your people' that it brings. There must be many hundreds of thousands of never-was netballers, golden oldies, tennis and touch footy players yearning for the social contact that has been an essential part of their lives. I just hope that they are all able to sustain some form of contact with their friends and comrades for their own wellbeing in the months ahead."

Me too. Gotta love this city.

NSW win a clock-stopper

Just before they shut the Queensland border, every state croquet team gathered in Brissie for the annual interstate clash. After five days of intense play, it all came down to the final game of the final match of the final day, with the NSW Women's team, led by Alison Sharpe, wrestling Victoria for the bikkies. Cometh the hour, cometh the women. For my friends, look! Right at the death, it is Alix Verge, a leading NSW player of great experience, tenacity and heart, striding out to do battle for the final chukka. (Editor, something like a chukka, anyway.) The match is going shot for shot, strike for strike, as she goes against her Vic opponent Elaine Coverdale for the magic figure of 26 hoops or whoever is ahead after 2 hours 45 minutes. As the sun begins to set, and the clock ticks down ever more loudly, neither player will yield. TIME! The clock stops. Everybody spins around to look at the scoreboard. Alix has come through again, and NSW vanquishes Victoria 11-9!

Yes, friends, let the record show: at the end of time, the mighty NSW Croquet women were the last left standing atop the winner's podium.

Kinloch an underarm-achiever

Australia's favourite sports historian Geoff Armstrong got back to me on the subject of who Australia's last accomplished underarm bowler was. And the answer is ...

John Kinloch! Come on down! He played three first-class matches for NSW against Victoria from 1859 to 1862, and played two games for NSW against HH Stephenson's England XI that toured Australia in 1861–62.

The Melbourne Age of 9 January 1862 described Kinloch the cricketer this way: "A fast under-hand bowler with good twist, and well on the wicket. Very dangerous to batsmen unacquainted with his style. A medium bat, and not remarkable in the field. "The great cricket journalist and commentator AG ‘Johnnie' Moyes once wrote: "John Kinloch was a star underarm bowler. A big man, he moved to the wicket with a stately and heavy tread, held the ball between thumb and two fingers, and propelled it with speed and accuracy. As he was near-sighted, Kinloch always wore a monocle when playing."

Our kind of bloke!

"One of the earliest graduates from Sydney University," Armstrong notes, "among the students he tutored was Edmund Barton, first prime minister of Australia."

All that, and 160 years on, from the days of his greatest glory, he was remembered in the pages of the Herald.

What they said

Un-named NRL staffer to Andrew Webster on news that Peter V'landys was not happy with the game's pandemic expert over the fact her predictions have not been borne out yet, and so he wants to restart the comp against her advice. "Only in rugby league could there be a war of words between the game's chairman and its own pandemic expert."

V'landys, explaining to 30 NRL players how things have come to the point that they will have to take pay cuts: "We've been eating champagne and caviar for years. But that's nobody's fault." I know.

Eric Dickerson, the LA Rams' vice president of business development unhappy with the new logo for the team, which was subsequently canned: "Someone said it looks like a penis; it did."

Ben Cousins in his tell-all interview on why he will do what he can not to go back to prison: "This time is going to be no different. It's going to be a challenge and I'm going to have to take it seriously. There's too much I've missed out on already. There's days and time that I can't get back, that my kids don't get back."

AOC rejects claims it colluded to postpone the Olympic Games: "Any assertions that a decision by the Australian Olympic Committee to tell its athletes to plan for a Tokyo Olympic Games in 2021 was done in concert with either the International Olympic Committee or the Canadian Olympic Committee (COC) are completely incorrect."

Rod Kafer: "We know rugby was sick a long time before the coronavirus came along. Rugby has been sick for years. People have been calling for change and it hasn't occurred. And yet the same people who have taken it off the cliff are still there, still employed." Unfair, Rod.

Dr Andrew Miller, President of the WA branch of the Australian Medical Association: "Horse racing should be put on hold to respect the rest of the community. It's very difficult to understand why you would send such mixed messages to the public when we're asking for everyone to stay at home at the moment."

Former Japanese Prime Minister Yukio Hatoyama in a tweet after the number of positive cases of COVID-19 in Japan shot up after the IOC postponed the Olympics: "In order to make an impression that the city was taking control of the coronavirus, Tokyo avoided making strict requests and made the number of patients look smaller. The coronavirus has spread while they waited. It was Olympics first, not Tokyo's residents."

IOC President Thomas Bach: "Humankind currently finds itself in a dark tunnel. These Olympic Games Tokyo 2020 can be a light at the end of this tunnel." ‘Cept it will be 2021.

Ben Calveley, the Lions managing director now that their 2021 tour of South Africa will clash with the Olympics: "We are determined to play our part in what will be an extraordinary summer of sport. There should not be any direct clashes with Lions matches and Olympic events given the time difference between South Africa and Tokyo, so fans should not miss out on any action. We are expecting a fantastic series against the world champions."

Team of the week

John Davies. Australia's only male breaststroke gold medallist died in Pasadena, late last month, aged 90. After swimming, he moved to the States, became a lawyer and was then appointed a District Judge by Ronald Reagan in 1986. Among other things he was the judge who presided over the trial of the Los Angeles Police Department officers charged with assaulting Rodney King!

RIP Tony Lewis. The mathematician of Duckworth-Lewis-Stern (DLS) fame passed away aged 78.

Wimbledon. Cancelled for this year.

Roger Federer and Serena Williams. Will we ever see them in a tennis grand slam event again? Martina Navratilova, for one, fears not.

Sports nostalgia. Making a huge comeback!
