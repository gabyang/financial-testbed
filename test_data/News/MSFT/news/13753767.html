Part Three: The Medium Is The Message

The philosopher and cultural critic Marshall McLuhan once wrote that “A point of view can be a dangerous luxury when substituted for insight and understanding.” As people continue to march across the United States and elsewhere crying out against racism, bigotry, and otherness, it behooves businesses to hear these voices and lend their own to this growing choir to be a powerful force for change that can have significant reverberations across the cultural landscape and transform the way we as a society can engage with social inequities. The responsibility of the marketing and advertising professional should not be seen as just a perfunctory role to help sell products and services, but rather a task that has a much deeper meaning. Marketing and advertising can be argued is a political and social activity that wields significant power in helping to shape meaning, drive perception, and provide a lexicon to help develop socially conscious organizations to empower real change in the behaviors of business and consumers.

We are in a time of transformation where the backdrops of our lives are being impacted by major disruption from social unrest to a pandemic. Perhaps with less distraction from sports, concerts, to limited mobility in our daily lives due to social distancing, this has given us the time to reevaluate what is truly important. Corporate social responsibility has been increasingly significant to all consumers, but especially to millennials. Over the years research has illustrated that millennials have a preference to not only do business that align themselves with brands with a social message, but one’s with ethical business practices. As consumers demand more accountability, it is time for the marketing and advertising professional to step forward and exercise bona fide leadership in helping organizations and their brands to highlight their inherent value as corporate citizens.

As the country celebrated Juneteenth this past week in the wake of protests we are reminded we have a long way to go. However, it ought to be said that there are companies such as Nike that used their brand as a way to not only raise consciousness by embracing Colin Kaepernick but realized the value of doing good business. It was this good business that led to selling out his “True 7” shoe on the first day of its North American release. Another example of the power of advertising was during the Superbowl this past February, Microsoft introduced its new Adaptive Xbox controller. The commercial showed how kids with disabilities can seamlessly participate with friends doing what kids do, having fun, and playing games together. While this ad was more subtle in tone than the Nike Kaepernick ad, it was just as revolutionary. Both through its placement and content, the ad demonstrated that while persons may have different abilities, they often are more alike than not and the ability to have a shared experience can be joyful and help us find a frame of reference for a greater co-existence.

This July marks the 30th anniversary of the signing of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), which can be argued is the most important Civil Rights bill since 1964. As much as Juneteenth is about emancipation, the ADA echoes that sentiment stating that persons with disabilities who make up 20% of the United States population should not be seen as others and should be given the access to fully participate in every facet of society.

In an age where social media and the internet as a whole has become the prevailing manner of communications, semiotics and imagery have taken their rightful place in remaking the world around us. Marketing and advertising professionals play a pivotal role in shaping this reality and helping organizations position themselves as key players in a socially conscious economy. These professionals hold a new social currency in that their acumen and leadership are essential in developing a new framework for doing social good while connecting this notion with the economic goals of the organization. In these changing times, those in the marketing and advertising business are tasked with a new mandate. To find a more humanistic approach and connect the importance of race, bias, and otherness with the reinvention of organizational goals and the growth of brand extension. In the next Mindset Matters column, we will examine how disability can bridge that divide and offer a more humanistic approach towards equity while continually reaching a diverse audience of consumers.
