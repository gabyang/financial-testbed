Here’s An Overview Of What’s New In Accessibility From Apple In 2020

Apple at WWDC this week took the wraps off a slew of new enhancements for its five software platforms: iOS, iPadOS, macOS, watchOS, and tvOS. There are the headliner additions—the home screen redesign in iOS 14 and the interface revamp in macOS Big Sur are amongst the most prominent—but accessibility, as usual, is just as noteworthy. It could even be argued that accessibility features are, in many ways, even more impressive and innovative than those that get top billing.

Whereas last year Voice Control and the AssistiveTouch pointer feature garnered most of the broader attention, this year Apple’s Back Tap and Sound Detection software are the two accessibility features to go mainstream in these early days of beta season.

Marques Brownlee of MKBHD fame on YouTube tweeted a short video showing how Back Tap works. The implementation is enchantingly clever; it is quintessential Apple, insofar as it is a classic interplay of hardware and software. It is as delightful and playful as it is useful.

The idea behind Back Tap is simple: For many with cognitive and/motor delays—or, let’s face it, anyone who appreciates whimsy—the ability to tap the back of one’s iPhone to perform certain, user-defined actions greatly reduces friction. A “back tap” can trigger Notification Center, for example, which alleviates the need to do a long vertical wipe on the screen. But it goes further than system functions—as Federico Viticci of MacStories noted on Twitter, Back Tap is also capable of launching shortcuts. This means users don’t need to tediously search out the Shortcuts app or widget or share sheet.

Like Voice Control (which received updates too) last year, Back Tap is remarkably well-considered and feature-rich for a first pass at a concept.

Much of Apple’s accessibility focus in 2020 centered on hearing, and the new Sound Detection feature is one fruit of their labor. What this feature does is alert you to certain ambient sounds—a fire alarm or crying baby are two possibilities—via a notification sent to one’s iPhone. This is obviously designed with the deaf and hard-of-hearing community in mind; especially in emergency situations, being notified of an alarm could literally be life-saving. It is a decidedly higher-tech take on using regular lightbulbs to alert a deaf person to the phone or doorbell ringing.

Here is a cursory look at a selection of other accessibility features coming to Apple’s operating systems later this year.

Headphone Accommodations. Headphone Accommodations is designed to amplify ambient sounds to make them easier to hear. As Apple describes it on their website: “This new accessibility feature is designed to amplify soft sounds and adjust certain frequencies for an individual’s hearing, to help music, movies, phone calls, and podcasts sound more crisp and clear.” The company also notes this feature also works with Transparency mode on AirPods Pro, saying it makes “quiet voices more audible and tuning the sounds of your environment to your hearing needs.” Headphone Accommodations works with the regular, second-generation AirPods as well.

Sign Language Recognition in Group FaceTime. A big one for RTT (Real Time Text) users: FaceTime now can detect when someone in a chat is speaking sign language. Members of the deaf and hard-of-hearing community have long gravitated towards Apple devices for video chat because of FaceTime. It will interesting to find out how, if at all, the software alerts a deaf or heard-of-hearing person to someone wanting their attention; waving and tapping are common ways of doing this.

VoiceOver Gets Smarter. Apple has applied its machine-learning technologies to make its VoiceOver screen reader smarter. The biggest addition is VoiceOver is now able to recognize key elements in third-party apps that do not have express support for accessibility. It makes otherwise inaccessible software accessible to blind and low vision users, but should not be seen as a crutch. This is indeed a good thing, but doesn’t absolve developers from doing the work to make their app accessible.

Elsewhere, VoiceOver has been improved such that it now can speak text it recognizes in images and photos. It also can speak complete sentences when reading aloud image descriptions on the web and in apps. VoiceOver also can detect an app’s controls to aid in navigation.

Magnifier Gets Redesigned. Magnifier, which Apple introduced with iOS 10 in 2016, has gotten a makeover. New features include the ability to capture multiple images at once, and multitasking support has been added on iPad.

Xbox Adaptive Controller for Apple Arcade. Especially useful on tvOS, new this year is support for Microsoft’s Xbox Adaptive Controller. This means Apple Arcade can be more inclusive, provided game developers support it in their apps.

Voice Control Improvements. Introduced last year to much fanfare, Voice Control has been made better in a few ways. It now supports British English and Indian English voices. In addition, VoiceOver commands now can used in conjunction with Voice Control commands; common ones include “read all” and “activate.” It’s also possible to have separate Sleep/Wake commands when using multiple devices at the same time.

Accessibility Setup on Apple Watch and More. As it did in iOS 13, Apple has added the ability to flag accessibility features in the Setup Buddy process in watchOS 7. The Extra Large watch face has been redesigned for better readability and now includes a complication. Lastly, the VoiceOver Rotor, which is a popular, gesture-based way to use VoiceOver, is coming to the Watch for the first time.
