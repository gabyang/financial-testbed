Patent Filings Roundup: Clash of Clans Conflict Continues; First ANDA-Related Pharmaceutical Patent Discretionary Denial; Freedom-to-Operate Action on Synthetic Peptide Tech

Jonathan Stroud

September 23, 2020, 01:15 PM   0

Share

Support IPWatchdogwith an individual sponsorship: Click here

District court patent filings bounced back this week to 86, while the Patent Trial and Appeal Board (PTAB) saw a slight jump to 44, fueled in part by eight under-the-wire covered business methods (CBM) filings just prior to that program’s expiry. WSOU Holdings filed another round against new defendants from their massive Nokia/Alcatel-Lucent portfolio, asserting new one-off patents not previously asserted, bringing their litigation total this year to 131, and bringing new defendants F5 Networksand Xilinx into the fray. It seems there may be no end to new suits in sight.

Pharmaceutical Hatch-Waxman Litigation Leads to First IPR Discretionary Denial:If you’re a patent attorney and you felt an earthquake last week but weren’t sure what caused it, it was this: in the first ANDA-related IPR proceeding for an Orange Book-listed patent, the Board denied the petition using their discretion under Section 314(a) and the Fintiv factors. The patent, owned by Janssen Pharmaceutica NV, a wholly owned subsidiary of Johnson & Johnson, is based on formulations of paliperidone palmitate, an injectable used to treat schizophrenia.

The drug has multiple abbreviated new drug application (ANDA)-related litigations pending, most notably by Teva and Mylan. The Board analyzed the petition under their Fintiv factors, but recognized the special administrative conflict inherent in ANDA cases that set them apart from traditional litigations, and which make stays particularly unlikely:

In the Mylan litigation … with the 30-month limit provided for by 21 U.S.C. § 355(j)(5)(B)(iii) expiring on January 2, 2022, we think it unlikely that a stay will be granted in the Mylan litigation, either.

It’s unfortunate it has taken anyone in the government this long to recognize and respond to that conflict; it’s my personal opinion that the PTAB has always had the ability to recognize the administrative and legislative conflict and deny institution where the Hatch-Waxman Act and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval process takes precedence, and that Fintiv unnecessarily curtails that discretion. That said, kudos to the panel for recognizing that parallel IPRs where legislation forces district court and regulatory approval processes is unavoidable, as opposed to traditional litigation, which is stayed some 70% of the time.

Clash of Clans War Rages On: As I’ve noted in the past, the gaming company Supercell Oy, which makes and markets the wildly popular “Clash of Clans” mobile game, has been going to war with Japanese game maker Gree Inc. Supercell has filed a whole phalanx of IPRs, many of which have been denied under Section 314 discretion, including more denied this week. Well, in the district court litigation, the judge narrowed the number of patents in dispute, and so the parties “settled” or withdrew a number of their petitions, while at the same time filing more on other patents. The game wars rage on.

Bicycle Therapeutics CLIPS Pepscan Patents:In what appears to be a wide-ranging freedom-to-operate action without pending litigation, UK innovator Bicycle Therapeutics has launched a European patent opposition, a German nullity action, and now U.S. inter partes reviews (IPR2020-01626 and -01569) against two U.S. patents owned by Dutch company Pepscan Systems B.V. over technology they both could compete over—methods of synthesizing peptide structures (or “macrocycles”). Bicycle’s proprietary “CLIPS” technology focuses on bi-cyclic peptide therapies for cancer, in that peptide structures are attached (or clipped, as it were) to toxic compounds and thus cancer cells can be zeroed in on with minimal collateral damage. It has been widely touted, and has the potential to be a breakthrough new form of biologic therapy—it’s effectively protein-mimicking technology that would allow targeted treatment—but it has a ways to go before widespread commercialization.
