It's not rock 'n' roll, but you should engage with your pension pot, says LEE BOYCE - otherwise, you could face a far poorer retirement

By LEE BOYCE FOR THISISMONEY.CO.UK

Updated: 05:00 GMT, 6 June 2019

  • e-mail
25 shares

It's a hard task getting people interested in pensions, especially those under the age of 35.

Retirement feels so far in the future for many living in the here and now. They pay in each month without being actively interested.

Meanwhile, confusing messages from politicians – including our housing minister, stating that younger generations could tap into their pensions to buy a home – alongside products such as the Lifetime Isa, muddy the water between the vastly different financial goals of owning a property and building a retirement pot.

We've all read those 'celebrity' money columns where they often say 'I don't have a pension, my pension is my property' – and the worry is, many could fall into this trap.

Jumpin' Jack Cash: Engaging with your pension may not feel rock 'n' roll, but figures suggest it is a worthwhile exercise and like the Rolling Stones it should keep you rocking for decades

Think of your pension like the Rolling Stones: in the early days you might struggle to envisage it decades into the future, but in nearly 60 year's time it will hopefully keep you rocking in old age.

Recently, I've been showing my pension much more love. The most basic move was to up contributions, prompted into action by compound calculators.

However, going one step further, is diversifying your pension.

Now bear with me. It may sound dull to those who don't have much appetite for financial matters, but not taking care here could penalise you in the future to the tune of thousands - if not, hundreds of thousands of pounds - all while investing in companies you may not like.

Figures from the Tax Incentivised Savings Association this week show 95 per cent of members of defined contribution schemes (which most younger workers have) are invested in the default fund.

That is a huge figure.

Personally speaking, up until last year, all of my retirement cash was in a default fund.

I'm not embarrassed to admit that I didn't do much with it, as it was chugging away nicely, plus a number of life events had pushed pension planning far down the pile of things to sort.

But last year, an annual statement indicating a dip in returns made me question what was going on – and where my money was actually being invested. I then questioned why I hadn't diversified.

Drilling into my default fund, nearly half of it is exposed to UK companies.

In the top 10 of firms my default fund invests in: HSBC, Shell, BP, British American Tobacco and Diageo.

Depending on your views – and especially in light of how environmentally conscious people are becoming – this might not be an acceptable blend to you.

Many people – when asked if they invest – will say no. But if you've got a private pension, you're an investor. So, if you have an objection to this kind of company, pay attention.

The reality is that if you don't touch your default fund, you could be invested in global firms you don't like without knowing it. If you do take an interest, there could be options that far better match your ethos.

So, I sat down and researched all the funds available to me via my provider in a bid to spread my eggs into different baskets.

One fund I am now buying as part of a blend, for example, invests heavily in North America, with the firms in the top ten including Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Facebook. Digital-focused firms that give my pension a bit more of a modern touch.

Now, back to that Tisa research. It found that default funds of the 20 largest pension providers delivered returns ranging between 3.4 and 11.9 per cent in the last three years.

What this could mean is a huge difference in what you could receive in retirement.

Tisa says a worker on a £30,000 salary invested in a pension fund with low annual growth of 3.4 per cent would have a pot worth £153,600 after 50 years.

Someone on the same salary invested in a pension scheme with a higher end 11.9 per cent annual growth rate, would see their fund valued at £2,271,200 in the same timeframe.

The magic of compounding at work once again – earning interest on your interest, or gains on your gains.

Recent research by financial information firm Defaqto also found a six percentage point difference between the top performing default fund over three years compared to the worst.

Savers can choose to not be in their default fund – or choose to partially invest in it, and spread the risk elsewhere.

The Pension Advisory Service says: 'It's generally a good idea to invest in several funds, so that you don't rely on the performance of one individual asset.'

I urge workers to log-in to your pension account and have a look at what you're invested in. Then at least have a look around to see what else is available.

Compare fund performance (this isn't an indicator of future returns, but still a useful benchmark), read the key investor information document and look at the risk rating.

It's worth pointing out here that you want to take note of the total expense ratio or ongoing charges figure too. In a nutshell, it is a measure of the total cost of a fund to the investor. The higher the percentage, the more costly it is to have it in your list of funds, so bear it in mind.

But to go back to my North America example, the TER is actually slightly lower than the default fund.

In its report, Tisa calls for people to take more interest in what the investment performance has been on the fund value over the long-term – most will invest for 40 years plus - and the difference between that and default funds to build a bigger pension pot.

One expert, William Burrows, retirement director at Better Retirement, told FT Adviser that it's 'vitally important people take an interest where their pensions are invested…there is a lot at stake.'

He goes on to add: 'An increasing number of people are choosing to self-select but most people are simply not equipped to make complex investment decisions.'

He recommends having an adviser to recommend a risk rated and well-diversified portfolio in line with long term objectives.

Some will be confident to go it alone, and diversify – but for others, a good financial adviser could help, and a small outlay near the beginning of your pension investment journey could be worth thousands in the future.

What to ask an adviser...

Ask an adviser if they are willing to review the 'walled garden' of funds in your employer's pension scheme for a one-off fee.

You will want them to suggest the most suitable ones for your age and goals, and how to split your contributions between them.

Bear in mind that staying in your employer's scheme means you benefit from its contributions into your pot along with your own.

If an adviser encourages you to open a self-invested personal pension (Sipp) through them, perhaps to sit alongside your work one, you could miss out on this free cash.

Many employers will match higher contributions you make - another reason to stick with your work scheme.
