Microsoft’s Winning Move Is Not Making The Surface Book 3 Your Ultimate Laptop

Microsoft’s recent Surface announcements have focused on defining new spaces in the ARM-powered Surface Pro X update; and bringing a popular line to a wider audience with the Surface Laptop Go. But what of the flagship? Launched in May this year, the Surface Book 3 is looking to be a bit of an outlier for Microsoft… a Surface device that doesn’t demand to redefine the space where it lives.

Don’t get me wrong, it's an utterly competent laptop with the same styling touches that helped the Surface Book stand out when the first iteration arrived in October 2015. But the PC world has moved on and the expectations of a high-end laptop for creatives have moved on. While the Surface Book has also moved on, the competition has moved further and faster since then.

I’ve spent some time with the larger 15-inch Surface Book 3, sporting the Intel’s tenth-generation i7-1065G7 processor, 32 GB of RAM, and Nvidia’s GeForce GTX 1660 Ti Max-Q graphics card. That’s close to the top end of the configurations - if you need more graphical power a model with the Quadra RTX 3000 Max-Q is available.

Let’s get something out of the way. In my day to day work, this is just a little bit too much power for me. I can definitely create my office documents and work on everything needed to get these words and pictures published on the internet; there’s more than enough oomph to do the straightforward editing for my Retro Gaming YouTube channel (as well as run the streaming software and the emulators in one place); editing and exporting audio podcasts is incredibly smooth; and it can happily stream audio and run radio playout software when I need to go live on the airwaves.

That said, the extra power allows me to use the Surface Book 3 as a solid yet unspectacular gaming machine. While it’s not designed specifically for this space - there’s no way the Surface Book 3 can keep up with the performance and thermal control in the likes of a Dell Alienware or Razer Blade gaming laptop - with some tweaks to each game’s settings you can reach 60 fps on current titles. You’ve also got the option of turning it all down to 1080p for more frames, even if this ignores most of the potential of the 3000 x 2000 pixel touchscreen.

Why is all this important? Because for many people, the Surface Book 3 could be all the computing power they need for the next few years, even if the laptop falls short at the very top of the performance tree.

The Surface Book 3 is effectively the same design as the first Surface Book launched five years ago. Yes there have been technical improvements to the likes of the hinge, the latching system that allows the screen to detach, and an increase in the keyboard base to accommodate more cooling for the graphics processor, but it’s clearly the same design.

And here lies the Surface Book 3’s achilles heel. There’s no room in the design to allow today’s top-end specifications that are needed by creatives. The defining feature of the detachable screen to offer a Windows 10 tablet means that the screen assembly has to act as a complete standalone Windows 10 machine. Given the expansive screen and the requirement to be as thin and light as possible, the design is limiting the technology that can be integrated.

The thicker form factor of the Surface Pro ultraportables allows for more thermal management (including a fan to help shift hot air out of the hardware. Thanks to some smart engineers, the extra graphical power is accommodated in the keyboard, which does offer more cooling potential thanks to the thicker wedge as you approach the hinge.

But the trade-offs remain. Take the processor. Microsoft is using a U-Series processor from Intel. This is designed for ultraportable machines and draws 15 watts. Laptops with more power are going to use the H-Series processors that can run up to 45 watts with more cores and threads (the aforementioned Razer Blade uses an eight-core sixteen thread Core i7-10875H).

If you want a detachable screen on your laptop, then you are going to have to sacrifice some computing power to do this.

The Surface Book 3 is not the ultimate laptop for those that need as much power as possible in a laptop. That doesn’t mean that those doing video editing, heavy lifting on images, or software development should ignore the Surface Book 3; but they should be aware that there are limitations at the very top of the envelope.

If your requirements are high but not quite as demanding, then the Surface Book 3 is a solid machine. While most people are going to keep it in the laptop mode, you can’t discount the tablet mode. This remains a significant part of the package and there will be users where the ability to lift the screen off and use the Surface Pen directly on the screen is the killer feature. That third mode - flipping the screen around and attaching it to the base creates a portable drawing desk with just enough rake on the screen to open up a different way of creating on a portable computer.

The trade-off between power and design is clearly on show here. Those looking for the design, the transformable modes of use, and the increased versatility this brings, are the target market for the Surface Book 3.

And as I noted earlier, the Surface Book 3 gives me more than enough power for the work I need my computer to do. When the world is allowed to travel again and I need to do all my reporting and broadcasting from a remote press room somewhere in Europe on a single machine, the Surface Book 3 will be a good choice.

Arguably the Surface Book invigorated PC design when it was launched. The comparisons to Apple’s MacBook Pro laptops continue to be made (and frankly I prefer the Surface’s detachable and touch-enabled screen over the removal of the function keys to add the touch bar on Apple’s macOS laptops) and the five year old design of the Surface Book still stands out in a crowd.

Microsoft’s partners have delivered new form factors and ideas to the Windows 10 table, and have caught up with the Surface Book and in many cases overtaken some elements of the package. In a sense that’s a win for Microsoft, because an invigorated Windows 10 ecosystem benefits everyone in the system. The Surface Book and Surface Pro range contributed to that by setting the bar.

Perhaps that's why I have a lingering sense of ‘is that it?’ with the Surface Book 3. The specs have been bumped up, it fits in with my work comfortably, it still has all the design cues and engineering that define the machine… but it hasn’t moved the bar forward or challenged the market. The Surface Book 3 does everything I need it to do, but I’m not sure it’s doing absolutely everything it could do for Microsoft.
