Bridgerton Had The Chance To Fix The Book’s Biggest Mistake, But Failed

Content warning: This article contains mentions of sexual assault and rape.

Netflix’s hot new show, Bridgerton, includes a controversial scene from the book that was a huge mistake and should have been left out. Bridgerton is based on a Regency Era romance novel, The Duke and I, by Julia Quinn. The Netflix series diverges from The Duke and I on several points, but that makes it even more confusing that one upsetting scene was included when it could (and should) have been removed.

✕ Remove Ads

In both the book and show, Daphne Bridgerton and Simon, the Duke of Hastings, stumble into a loving friendship that turns into a forced marriage after they are discovered alone together. Before their wedding, Simon tells Daphne that he can’t have children, but this doesn’t dissuade Daphne from marrying him. The problem is that Simon wasn’t being entirely truthful. He is, in fact, physically capable of procreating, but he made a vengeful vow to his dying father that their family line would end with him. Daphne, who is completely ignorant of how sex and procreation work, doesn’t find out that Simon’s use of the pull-out method is what’s keeping her from having a baby until after they’re married. She finds someone to explain what her mother wouldn’t and, armed with this knowledge, does something awful.

✕ Remove Ads

In the book, Simon returns home drunk one night and Daphne realizes that she can do what she wants with him. While Simon is still drunk and not fully conscious, Daphne manages to arouse him and, to be very clear, rapes her husband. Readers were understandably upset by the unfortunate inclusion of this harmful romance trope and the scene was markedly changed in the show. In Bridgertonseason 1, episode 6, "Swish," Simon is neither drunk nor asleep and Daphne’s decision seems more premeditated, but Bridgerton mostly glosses over Daphne's horrific act and pretends that Simon and Daphne are equally at fault for the state of their marriage following that night.

✕ Remove Ads

Later in the same episode, Lady Whistledown’s omniscient narration indicates that Daphne’s means were “wretched," except Lady Whistledown was actually writing about Marina Thompson. Though it's implied to the audience that Whistledown is also referring to Daphne, it’s not enough. Bridgerton needed to show that Daphne was wrong by depicting meaningful consequences for her actions. Instead, Daphne sobs when she starts menstruating, realizing that Simon may now leave her, and the scene comes off as sympathetic to Daphne. But it’s hard to feel sorry for a character that only seems to feel sorry for herself. Regardless of whether Daphne knew that it was wrong or whether Simon felt that he had been the victim of sexual assault, there needed to be some restitution; Daphne needed to recognize her actions for what they were (sexual assault), apologize to Simon (which she never did), and accept that he’s not obligated to forgive her.

✕ Remove Ads

But the scene also speaks to another frustrating and problematic romantic trope: lack of communication. It would have been truly refreshing to see Daphne (Phoebe Dynevor) earnestly ask Simon (Regé-Jean Page) why he lied to her and then the two might have bonded over their mutual understanding of loss and the pressures of society - or they might have realized that their toxic relationship was unsustainable and parted ways. Instead, Bridgertonperpetuates the absurd idea that the only way to tell interesting, dynamic, and sexy stories about a committed couple is to have them constantly lying to and hurting one another. It makes for predictable and irritating television on top of the fact that it's a poor model for impressionable viewers who might start thinking that being lied to and hurt is normal or even romantic.

✕ Remove Ads

What’s also frustrating is how obviously wrong Daphne’s actions would be considered if her and Simon’s genders were reversed – not that it should matter. Sexual assault is sexual assault. And in a world where male victims of sexual assault are not only treated with less seriousness and sensitivity than women but often (and appallingly) played for laughs, a progressive and otherwise sex-positive show like Bridgerton should have known better and handled the scene differently or, better yet, left it out.
