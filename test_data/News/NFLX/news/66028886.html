In ‘The Serpent’ Jenna Coleman Portrays A Woman Willing To Kill For Love

It’s been said that the worst lies are the lies we tell ourselves. For Marie-Andrée Leclerc, the girlfriend of serial killer Charles Sobhraj, this couldn’t be more true.

Jenna Coleman is riveting in her portrayal of Leclerc in the Netflix/BBC co-production The Serpent, which is premiering stateside April 2 on Netflix. The eight-episode limited series is already a critically-acclaimed sensation in the UK and it deserves all the praise it’s receiving.

Inspired by shocking true events, the series unravels the complicated story of Sobhraj and the remarkable attempts to bring him to justice. The pair traveled across Thailand, Nepal and India in 1975 and 1976 where they would target and trap their victims. They preyed on travelers exploring the "hippie trail" of Southeast Asia and once ensnared in their web of lies, there was little hope of escape.

In a recent phone interview, Coleman described how she was able to glean so much about this extremely complicated woman from her own words. “I read her diaries and they were so useful to me in my research. I was able to get more of a glimpse into her psychology. She lived in such a world of delusion and this was to protect herself. She chose and created this reality and lived like this character.”

Coleman learned just how obsessed Leclerc was with Sobhraj. “This explained so much about how she operated in the world. She was so tormented. Her life before meeting Charles was a dull gray and banal. She was craving something in order to feel alive. It was better to live in color no matter how dark those colors were. They were better than the gray.”

Leclerc, she explains, never left Canada before her fateful trip to India in 1975. She met Sobhraj on the way over and he became her tour guide for the duration of her trip. Before returning home to Quebec, she promised she would visit him again. Three months later they met in Bangkok. “Within three weeks she was drugging people,” says Coleman.

Back to those lies people tell themselves; Leclerc was a willing participant in drugging tourists to steal their money and passports, but she turned a blind eye to the fact Sobhraj was also murdering them.

In one pivotal scene for Coleman, Sobhraj is in the living room of their shared apartment torturing his latest victims. Leclerc runs into their bedroom and blasts music so she can avoid hearing the terrifying scene play out right outside her door. Her ability to tune out the parts of life that she couldn’t face was a piece of this woman’s character that Coleman tuned into.

“This was how I approached portraying her; I’d think about all the suppression and drowning out of the background noise she’d create so she could live this exotic life with this adventurous man she loved. At her core, her self-worth was eroded and she was so fragile.”

Coleman studied Leclerc’s diaries in an effort to really get into her head. “I discovered how it all began between them. She actually chased him. She was absolutely desperate. There was an entry in her diaries where she said something to the effect of only being able to breathe and live because of Charles. Her every waking thought was of him and the worse he treated her, the more addicted she was to him.”

This, adds Coleman, was a very dangerous tightrope Leclerc walked. “Charles became her god and this explains so much about her.”

Coleman tells me that when it comes to preparing for a role, it’s not as important for her to like a character as it is for her to find a way to understand the character. “My judgment of her is irrelevant. I was more interested in understanding and exploring her. Marie loved Charles and that was her religion. This was really key for me.”

Leclerc would deny any knowledge of the murders but many have said over the years that she had to have known. When asked what she thinks, Coleman concurs. “On a subconscious level she knew but she would not allow this into her conscious mind until the point where she couldn’t live in that illusion anymore. That was when it all unraveled and fell apart.”

In the end, Leclerc lost everything for a love that was, in many ways, a figment of her imagination. Once she realized the manipulation and psychological games Sobhraj played with her, her world crumbled around her.

The couple, alongside two new accomplices, would finally get caught in July of 1976 in New Delhi when they attempted to poison and rob French students. Sobhraj miscalculated things when he tried to poison the entire group at the same time and the drugs took effect sooner than expected. He was overpowered by his victims this time and the police were called.

Sobhraj committed at least a dozen murders. He and Leclerc were convicted and imprisoned in India. A few years after his release, he returned to Nepal in 2003 where he was arrested again and is currently serving a life sentence.

While in prison, Leclerc continued to deny any knowledge of the murders. Within three years, she was diagnosed with ovarian cancer. She was then allowed to return home to Canada where she died in 1984 at 38 years-old.

Coleman has heard the gamut of reactions to this complex woman. “It’s so interesting to hear how people respond to her. I find her and her psychology so fascinating. She was this very enigmatic and mysterious woman. She was a hidden woman. Her inner life was filled with such turmoil.”
