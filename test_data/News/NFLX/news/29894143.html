The Netflix docuseries How to Fix a Drug Scandal focuses on the misconduct of Massachusetts drug analysts Annie Dookhan and Sonja Farak, who were responsible for identifying evidence in criminal cases. Directed by Erin Lee Carr, the four-part investigation spotlights the legal consequences of using unreliable drug evidence in court, and how a miscarriage of justice was perpetuated by the Massachusetts State's Attorney Office.

✕ Remove Ads

In How to Fix a Drug Scandal on Netflix, a hired actress portrays Farak during recreated moments from unsealed court transcripts. Attorneys and journalists establish the context for Farak's 2013 arrest via on-camera interviews, and also discuss Farak's nine-year handling of over 30,000 pieces of drug-related evidence at a state drug lab in Amherst. Not long before the Farak scandal broke, Dookhan admitted to fabricating test results ("drylabbing") over several years at Hinton State Laboratory Institute in Boston.

Aside from covering the two main subjects, the Netflix docuseries focuses on the work of Luke Ryan, a defense attorney who was misled about crucial evidence. Incidentally, he didn't have the necessary information to properly defend clients who'd been wrongfully convicted. Here are the biggest reveals from How to Fix a Drug Scandal on Netflix.

✕ Remove Ads

Sonja Farak Was A Groundbreaking High School Football Player

How to Fix a Drug Scandal portrays Farak as a talented and highly-ambitious woman who struggled with the monotony of her daily work, and ultimately tampered with evidence to fuel her addiction. In the Netflix docuseries, Farak admits that she "turned out a certain way"despite a"normal childhood"in Newport, Rhode Island. Farak's mother, Linda, states that her daughter wasn't just a typical high school athlete - she was the first girl in Rhode Island to play high school football in a public school system, which was a groundbreaking achievement.

✕ Remove Ads

Sonja Farak Produced And Smoked Crack Cocaine At Work

Much of How to Fix a Drug Scandal involves establishing when Farak began tampering with drug evidence at work. During court proceedings, Superior Court C. Jeffrey Kinder established an exact "breaking bad" date as July 12, 2012, which meant that defense attorneys couldn't challenge any cases before the established time-frame. However, evidence that was later uncovered reveals that Farak began using drugs three months into her job, beginning in 2004. Farak initially used meth to feel more "alert," and later began using powder cocaine that she was able to access from large seizures. In a tragic twist, Farak began producing and smoking crack cocaine at her work station and thus spiraled out of control.

✕ Remove Ads

Annie Dookhan Catfished A Prosecutor

For years, Dookhan was known as the "superwoman" of the Hinton lab. According to the Netflix docuseries, she began "drylabbing" in order to please prosecutors. For some extra attention, she falsely claimed to be attending night school at Harvard University, and also created a fake e-mail account to romantically lure prosecutor George Papachristos. Essentially, Dookhan conversed with herself in e-mail exchanges to give the impression that she was divorced and "desirable," and then forwarded messages to Papachristos under an assumed identity.

Luke Ryan Exposed The Attorney General’s Office

Ryan filed several motions to inspect three boxes of files that were found in Farak's trunk, but was repeatedly denied by Assistant Attorney General Kris Foster because the documents weren't viewed as exculpatory evidence. After Farak was sentenced, Ryan gained access to the boxes and learned that the chemist has been using drugs dating back to 2004. The Netflix docuseries ends with a revelation from "The Carey Report," which describes the evidence cover-up as a "fraud upon the court." Over 35,000 cases were dismissed based on Farak and Dookhan's misconduct.

Farak And Dookhan Understood How To Fake A Drug Scandal

✕ Remove Ads

By skimming "five percent" from large drug seizures, Farak was able to fuel her addiction while working in relative secrecy. She figured out how to be assigned to specific cases, and had direct access to several types of drugs. The Netflix docuseries implies that nobody would have known about Farak's evidence tampering if she would have stayed alert and productive. She received an 18-month prison sentence and five years probation.

As for Dookhan, How to Fix a Drug Scandal on Netflix suggests that she had the intelligence and motivation to be a good employee, but chose to fabricate results to climb up the ladder more quickly as a female, first-generation immigrant. In 2013, she was given a sentence of three to five years in prison. Overall, the Netflix docuseries reaches the conclusion that a legal blueprint wasn't in place to deal with the two drug scandals, which slowed up the process and kept innocent people in jail.
