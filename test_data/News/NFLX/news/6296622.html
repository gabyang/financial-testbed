Billionaire Fugitives, Netflix’s Renewed Hope And Bloomberg Trolls Trump

Between the recent holidays, I couldn’t get enough of a few things: Lost in Space on Netflix, a murder mystery novel called The Hunting Party—and a real-life story with all the elements of a fictional thriller, Carlos Ghosn’s escape from Japan.

The details about the wealthy ex-Nissan CEO's getaway are still pretty scarce. (He reportedly stowed away in a box meant for concert equipment aboard a private jet that took him first to Turkey and then to Lebanon.) But his bolt for freedom got me and my editor, Luisa Kroll, wondering about what had happened in the past when other corporate titans turned fugitive.

I dug into the Forbes archives and other document vaults, like the cache of FBI files released in 2016 about the agency’s hunt for Marc Rich, the oil baron indicted on more than 60 charges in 1983 and pardoned by Bill Clinton 18 years later. “The ultra-rich often play by their own rules,” says Kroll, who has spent the better part of two decades reporting and editing stories about the world's wealthiest people. “Carlos Ghosn is just the latest in a string of rich fugitives who have gotten out and gotten away with it, a number of whom continue to live more than comfortable lifestyles abroad.”

These fugitive tales are not ones that conclude quietly. And it wouldn’t surprise me if, in a year or two, I spend those mellow moments from Christmas to New Year's watching the Netflix version of Ghosn’s story. However it ends.

— Abram Brown
