    Synopsis

    People can use Netflix on their mobile phone or TVs, by downloading or streaming and the shows that we have like, Altered Carbon, Sacred Games and a special filmed in Mumbai on the underworld means there is so much more exciting content coming.

    By Saurabh Bhattacharya

    In town for the ET GBS, Reed Hastings, the co-founder, Chairman and CEO of Netflix talks to EconomicTimes.com about business in India, his view on India’s content creators and the possibility of creating regional content. Edited excerpts:

    Economic Times (ET): How do you see business in India evolving in the last 2-3 years as digital mode of entertainment picks up in the country?
    Reed Hastings (RH):The great thing for Indian consumers is how the Internet is exploding with the data costs coming down, which means Netflix is being used more and more. People can use Netflix on their mobile phone or TVs, by downloading or streaming and the shows that we have like, Altered Carbon, Sacred Games and a special filmed in Mumbai on the underworld means there is so much more exciting content coming.

    ET: How does Netflix look at competition like Amazon Prime, which is global in nature and ALTBalaji, which is local in nature?
    RH: I am an Amazon Prime member and subscribe to HBO, which means a lot of people subscribe to a multiple services because they have different content. On Netflix you have The Crown, which is not available on any other service and all of the shows that we have is quite unique to Netflix and it gives a great experience.

      ET: How keen is Netflix to create local content and perhaps in regional languages?
      RH: For now we are focused on Hindi and English and having great success in that. We just launched a new movie called Love Per Square Foot, which is a classic Bollywood movie and is much fun. The amazing thing is it is doing well in India, but is also doing well for us in the UK, US, Mexico and it is actually about exporting Indian culture and giving the rest of the world a sense of this great Indian film making tradition.

      ET: Bollywood is often seen as India’s soft power, but you also have parts of regional cinema and content doing very well globally. Given that, what is your plan for the next 2-3 years?

      RH:       In the short-term, we are focused on Hindi-English combination and mastering that and getting good at that. After that we will experiment with more regional languages and broad range of different content available in India.

      ET: What is your take Indian startup ecosystem and especially in the content space? Do you see a difference in the quality of content vis-à-vis what you perhaps see in a country like the US?
      RH: It has never been richer. Prices are up with Netflix, Hotstar, YouTube all bidding so it is a great time to be a content producer in India. Indian movies have always been of very high quality, but Indian television has a lower production value with many advertisements. With Netflix there is no advertising so it is more like a DVD or the movie theater, which is a more pristine experience. So, there is a wide variety, but we have been very pleased with the Indian creative community.

      ET: India vs China. Where do you see a bigger market for paid content?
      RH:I think it is much more in India. It is open, vibrant market with a wider base of influences and because of that we are doing a lot of Indian content and exporting it around the world.
