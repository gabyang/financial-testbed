Lynette Fay: Jennifer Aniston's turning 50, so she'll be judged even more of a failure

The use of the word ‘spinster’ is so much more derogatory than ‘bachelor’, don’t you think? Why is a successful, single woman like Jennifer Aniston to be pitied whereas George Clooney, when he was single was the most eligible bachelor in the world?

Lynette Fay

Lynette is an award winning presenter and producer, working in television and radio. Hailing from Dungannon, Co Tyrone, she is a weekly columnist with The Irish News.

By Lynette Fay
February 09, 2019 at 1:00am GMT

I SHED a tear when the final episode of the hit US comedy Friends aired in 2004. During the series' 10-year run, I had formed a very close relationship with Monica, Phoebe, Chandler, Joey, Ross and Rachel.

From ‘Smelly Cat’ to ‘he’s your lobster’ to ‘we were on a break’, many of the series' catchphrases entered the global vernacular.

Now, thanks to streaming services and a deal with Netflix worth $100 million, Friends is the most streamed series in the world. It’s both frightening and thrilling that my eldest niece, who's 17, is now as big a fan as I was of the trials and tribulations of the six 20-something regular customers of the Central Perk coffee shop.

Rachel Green was always my favourite. I wanted to be Jennifer Aniston. I still do. Back in the day, I craved ‘The Rachel’ haircut. I loved Rachel’s clothes too. Then Jennifer Aniston married Brad Pitt and the world proclaimed that she had hit the jackpot. The looks, the job, the man – she ‘had it all’.

Jennifer and Brad famously divorced. She has since married (Justin Theroux) and divorced again. She is one of the most scrutinised women in the world.

This week, she turns 50. Poor Jennifer.

Why is one of the most successful, fulfilled, creative and coincidentally beautiful women in the world rendered ‘a failure’?

In 2016, as a result of media speculation that she was pregnant, she addressed the media and societal critique of women in an open letter entitled ‘For the Record’, which was published by the Huffington Post.

In this brilliant open letter she called out the objectification and scrutiny of women and the "warped way we calculate a woman’s worth". That worth tends to be calculated by a wedding band and children. A woman is incomplete if she isn’t a wife and a mother.

Reaching an age milestone prompts the inevitable evaluation of life. What defines success and what trail, if any, are we blazing for young women who might look to us for example?

The ‘ambition’ to get married is not something I was brought up with. Reflecting now on my 20s and 30s, it was all around me. Many women my age couldn’t wait to get married or to have children, or both – good for them.

In retrospect, this societal expectation and pressure led me to misjudge what I really wanted. I had myself convinced that I was unhappy because neither had happened for me. Wrong. I should have been focussing on myself.

How many people get married without really knowing and understanding who they are because it’s what they ‘should’ be doing?

Unlike the famous line from the film Jerry Maguire, we shouldn’t need a partner or a child to complete us. Surely anything extra in our lives complements that which already exists?

Marriage and motherhood are experiences I observe from the outside in. I see both sides of it. I am 41. I’m single and I don’t have children. That may or may not change in the future, but at present it’s where I am in life. I know that I’m judged for it. This used to bother me. It doesn’t any more.

I understand where Jennifer was coming from when she wrote her letter. Whether women don’t marry by choice or by circumstance, why does society judge this position so cruelly?

The use of the word ‘spinster’ is so much more derogatory than ‘bachelor’, don’t you think? Why is a successful, single woman like Jennifer Aniston to be pitied whereas George Clooney, when he was single was the most eligible bachelor in the world? The answer is, she’s not.

The other derogatory term is ‘childless’. A woman is incomplete if she isn’t a mother.

More and more women are choosing not to have children. This is a very emotive subject on many levels. Some see this decision as a selfish one, given that so many women who dearly want to have children can’t. The experience of fertility treatment is not one I have had but I know that it is the cause of emotional and physical turmoil for couples and single people who pursue this course.

Sadly, women tend to be the harshest critics of other women. Does anyone ‘have it all’? Perhaps we should stop buying into this idea and live and let live, and as Jennifer Aniston puts it very eloquently, "determine our own ‘happily ever after’ for ourselves".

Happy 50th, Jen.
