Game On: An exclusive talk with 'Where the Heart Leads' developer Armature
Fly Intel
Sam Martinelli - email
Says Sony a "fantastic partner"

For this week's edition of "Game On," The Fly spoke in an exclusive interview with two members of Armature Studio, an independent developer that recently released narrative adventure game "Where the Heart Leads" for PC, PlayStation 4 (SONY), and PlayStation 5. In the interview, producer Greg John and creative director Todd Keller discussed the challenges of the adventure genre, the studio's relationship with Sony, their thoughts on cloud streaming, and more.

'WHERE THE HEART LEADS': Armature released "Where the Heart Leads," a game that involves many branching narrative choices and endings, last month to mixed or average reviews, according to review aggregator Metacritic. When asked how the company feels about the critical reception and overall number of reviews, Greg John said he's been "really happy" with the coverage. "For an indie game, we’ve gotten nearly 70 independent reviews," John told The Fly. "They’ve averaged in the 70s range. We’re really quite happy with the coverage and exposure of the title. I think it’s a very unique title, because it’s this narrative adventure game that started off as an oil painting, and we wrapped a narrative element into it. I think it’s a very unique experience. We were concerned it was going to be a ‘love it or hate it’ type game, but we’re quite happy that a lot of the people who reviewed it actually do love it.”

Keller noted that some reviews even said that "Where the Heart Leads" helped them think differently about how to play choice-based narrative titles, even saying it was somewhat of a "game changer." “That was most important to us," Keller said. "We knew it would be a somewhat polarizing game in terms of how you felt about the story or its outcomes. It’s probably a difficult game to review because it has many different endings and many different paths. On your first playthrough as a reviewer, if you didn’t like what [ending] you got or if it didn’t resonate with you, then your review is going to reflect that instead of the wide berth of choices and outcomes. So it’s probably different in that way as well.”

Keller added that the studio doesn't have "all the numbers yet" in terms of the game's sales so far.

NARRATIVE GAMES:Greg John said that the studio tried to make "Where the Heart Leads" stand out in a competitive space by focusing on a unique art style and by "really delivering on the narrative branching aspect of it." "I think we provide a very large [range] of possibilities for the player, because we set out not so much to create one narrative line, but the entire narrative line of somebody’s life," he said. "We wanted to make sure that lift felt like a real life, like it was rich and had a lot of choices and that those choices really mattered and took you down very different paths. If you play it once through, you’re seeing a very small part of what happens.”

“I agree with all that," Todd Keller said on the matter. "One of the challenges might be that there’s a certain style to narrative games that you expect when you play them. The pacing of this one and the way it’s more of a 3D open realm is a little bit different from other games. So I would say the challenge was to make a narrative game wrapped in an adventure game in a 3D roaming experience and make that appealing to players who are more used to point-and-click based adventure games. And the game is more open-ended as well, with players able to put their own personality into the game’s narrative choices.”

“One of the key things we tried to make sure of is that we were not judging the player for their choices," John added. "We don’t tell them there’s a right or wrong path. We tried to make it so that everything is your choice, and you can choose to do what you want, but we’re not judging which ones you make. The choices have consequences, but we don’t tell you they’re right or wrong.”

SONY RELATIONSHIP:When asked if the studio plans to release "Where the Heart Leads" on any other consoles or platforms, John said they couldn't talk about any future plans, but did mention that Sony was a "fantastic partner" during the whole process. "This is our third or fourth project with [Sony], and they’re a fantastic partner," the producer said. "They give us a ton of support from beginning to end. And we really like the PlayStation platform.”

"Honestly, working with Sony has been one of the best partnerships I’ve had in my career, even before we started this company," Keller added. "I also personally really like the PlayStation platform. I’ve always been a PlayStation owner from the beginning.”

When asked why in particular they enjoyed working with Sony so much, both John and Keller responded that they appreciate the console maker's focus on overall game quality over anything else. “For me, it’s coming from the past, when the PlayStation started, the types of games they had and developed, the ones they funded themselves - those games set what I really like about gaming and gave me just more of a passion for the types of games that could be created," the creative director said. "I think they’re in the forefront of technology as well, in terms of creating the devkits we use and everything that goes into PlayStation graphics.”

“Games sell platforms," John added. "I think Sony really understands that, so they make sure their games are very high quality. [As far as] tech goes, it’s a wonderful platform to develop on. Various consoles in the past have been very difficult to develop on, but the PS4 and PS5 are great to develop on, at least from our viewpoint.”

SUBSCRIPTION/STREAMING:On the topic of subscription services, Greg John told The Fly that services like Xbox Game Pass (MSFT) are "really great" because they give players "a ton of choices" without much of a commitment. Keller also said he looks subscriptions as well, though he particularly highlighted PlayStation Now's approach to streaming. "There are a lot of games from the past that [are hard to play now], but you can at least stream those games now and play them again," he said. "As far as what you get out of subscriptions, it depends on the quality of titles that you get for free. Everybody offers free games you can download every month, so it depends on what you actually get. I would judge a service by the quality of the games you see.”

"I think, and I’m not sure if this will be the truth or not, but I think things like streaming services will be the future, just like [in other media]," Greg John said on the future of game streaming. "We don’t buy DVDs anymore, we just watch Netflix (NFLX), right? In the end, once all the kinks are worked out, streaming gaming will probably be the future.”

FUTURE PLANS:In addition to creating original games like "Where the Heart Leads," Armature has done work in the past on developing next-generation ports of Sega's (SGAMY) "Bayonetta" and even working on downloadable content or other co-development work for games like "ARK: Genesis" and even Epic Games' "Fortnite." When asked if the studio's focus will shift more heavily towards original IP development if "Where the Heart Leads" sells well enough, John said that Armature always tries to have a "pretty diverse" portfolio of projects.

"We like doing ports. We think it’s a great thing for our engineers to experience different engines, and it’s a very interesting challenge and problem to do. We love doing co-development," John told The Fly. "We’re working with Riot. We’re working with Epic. They allow us to work on titles that require hundreds of people, and we simply can’t field hundreds of people but we can be part of thes AAA experiences through co-development. And of course, we love doing our own games too. We always try to have one original title going at any one time. Still, we are waiting to see how ‘Where the Heart Leads’ does, and in a perfect world, we would reroll the team to do another narrative or action-adventure game, trying to use all the infrastructure, tools, and learnings from this project and carry them over to the next project.”

"The reason we have so many different types of teams going on is not only for the viability of the company and keeping employees interested in what they’re doing, but also so that everybody is able to grow their skills," Keller noted. "You move from team to team and you’re able to experience a different type of product and production, and work with different kinds of partners, like Sony. Our internal people get to sort of pick and choose and feel out their careers for how they’re moving forward.”

“We’ve done that for a really long time, have three different styles of approaches," Keller added. "The most risky is obviously original games. It’s very difficult to make original IP. We all come from original IP backgrounds, but making an original IP that is highly advantageous from a money side of things is difficult. So we disperse teams appropriately and spread people out across multiple disciplines for whatever projects we have at any given time. We typically have 3-5 projects going on all at once.”

Investors in Epic Games include Tencent (TCEHY), KKR (KKR), Disney (DIS), and Sony.
