Safe stocks to own in a coronapocalypse

Currently, the stock market is in a weird situation where most stocks have exposure to the systemic risk of COVID-19.  As it is highly likely that social distancing will become the new norm, a long list of businesses will be badly hurt: airlines, restaurants, movie theatres, music events, conventions, lenders, etc.

If the COVID-19 situation drags on for 3+ years, you don’t want your portfolio to blow up because of it. It would make sense to put a good portion of your portfolio into less risky investments that will survive. Because individual stocks can blow up, it is a good idea to diversify (e.g. 10-30+ stocks). It’s also a good idea to avoid owning too many stocks with similar risks (e.g. stocks in the same industry, too much COVID-19 risk, etc.).

Stocks discussed: CNC, UNH, CHTR / LBRDA, TSN, HRL, COST, PGR, V, MA, MCO, SPGI, FB, GOOGL, IAC / MTCH, VRSN.

The criteria

These are the criteria that I feel are important for reducing risk:

  1. Won’t be hurt by COVID-19 if the situation drags on for 3 years.
  2. Not overvalued.
  3. Not in a dying industry.
  4. Earnings will grow in the future.
  5. Has some type of competitive advantage or something that protects the company against competition.

The first few criteria are obvious. The Dot-Com Bubble from 1999/2000 taught investors not to buy overpriced tech stocks, even though some of them such as Amazon and Google did incredibly well following the Dot-Com bust.

As far as the last 2 criteria go, I have a highly unconventional opinion about owning stocks that will grow their earnings significantly.  The idea of owning such stocks is to have good returns.  If your portfolio has poor returns, then you could lose the battle against inflation.  Underperforming the S&P 500 could be considered to be a risk.  In terms of bankruptcy risk, the higher quality companies tend to be less risky.  A McDonald’s restaurant is less likely to go under than a restaurant featured in Kitchen Nightmares(a reality TV series where Gordon Ramsay tries to turn around failing restaurants).  When an industry has fierce competition, the strongest players tend to last much longer than the weakest players.  You want high-quality businesses that are more profitable, grow quickly, and have much lower chances of declaring bankruptcy.

In the long run, most of the investment gains in a diversified portfolio come from dynamic companies that grow their earnings.  If you buy a basket of many stocks, a few of them will do exceptionally well and a handful of them will do exceptionally poorly.  The ones that do poorly will actually have very little effect on your portfolio’s returns while the superstars will determine almost all of your portfolio’s performance.  If you backtest a random basket of 30+ stocks, you will generally find this pattern.  One quick way to check that idea is to read about the “Nifty Fifty”, a group of overpriced growth stocks from the 1970s.  This group outperformed the S&P 500 and most of the outperformance was due to one stock out of the fifty.  So, to outperform the S&P 500, you more or less have to maximize your chances of finding the superstar stocks that grow their earnings consistently over a long period of time.

Why competitive advantages matter

Because the business world is competitive, most businesses cannot consistently grow at a very rapid rate. Competition will inevitably eliminate the unusually profitable opportunities. There are a few lucky businesses where this is not the case. They have some type of unique competitive advantage that protects them from competition such as a monopoly, brand, technology, or better management. Some of these advantages such as better management and fashionable brands aren’t very durable so we can never quite be sure if those companies will continue to be superstars- this makes diversification important.

“Safe”

It is very normal for businesses to go bankrupt. All sorts of crazy things can and will happen. For example, E-Trade Financial would have fit all of my criteria in 2002-2007. Unfortunately for its investors, the stock was terrible because E-Trade made huge investments in subprime mortgages. While it’s easy to see that E-Trade was a bad stock with the benefit of 20/20 hindsight, it would have been incredibly difficult to forecast its demise unless you were an expert in how mortgage backed securities and mortgage lending worked. This is why I emphasize diversification.

However, diversification will not protect you against many risks.  It will not protect you against everybody selling risky assets indiscriminately during a stock market crash.  If there is a massive stock market crash, it probably won’t matter what stocks you own.  Almost all stocks go down dramatically in a market crash.  This is a systemic risk with all stocks as there is a good chance that some investors in all stocks will panic at the same time.  But, diversification will protect you against some risks.

Not safe

Do not buy:

  • Chinese stocks listed solely on non-mainland China exchanges (e.g. Hong Kong, US, etc.). All of them are frauds, with the possible exception of the SOEs (state owned enterprises).
  • Marijuana stocks. All of them were masterminded by career stock promoters and scammers.
  • Startup bubble stocks like UBER, SHOP, etc. at their current sky-high valuations.
  • Tech bubble stocks like NVDA, AMD, TSLA, etc. at their current sky-high valuations. At lower valuations these are ok.

Also do not buy:

  • Oil E&Ps. The industry constantly destroys shareholders’ money.
  • Mining stocks. Both the senior and junior miners destroy shareholders’ money, causing the industry to underperform physical gold (e.g. pull up a chart of GDX or GDXJ versus GLD).

Stocks with low COVID-19 risk

Supermarkets and food companies that sell to them

Because COVID-19 is causing a (temporary?) shift from restaurants to supermarkets, companies that supply restaurants will do poorly while companies that mainly supply supermarkets will do well. Because everybody must eat, these are clearly essential businesses that will not see their revenues go away.

However, there is some risk with conventional chicken/pork/beef products.  Meat packaging facilities have turned out to be dangerous workplaces where COVID-19 spreads extremely quickly.  A CNN Business article notes a 25% reduction in meat capacity so far:

The United Food and Commercial Workers International Union, which represents more than 250,000 meatpacking and food processing workers, said on Thursday at least 13 processing plants have closed over the past two months, resulting in a 25% reduction in pork slaughter capacity and 10 percent reduction in beef slaughter capacity.

While the food supply chain is essential, it’s not clear if it will run at full capacity.  We haven’t yet figured out a solution for the danger of meat processing facilities, where many workers are indoors and standing close to one another.  (I hold the unconventional opinion that the accumulation of aerosols in an indoor environment is how most cases of COVID-19 are spread.)  If the Western world does not implement a solution for this problem (e.g. a very hard Wuhan-style lockdown, more restrictive than what Italy implemented), then conventional meat companies will see a hit to their revenues while COVID-19 is ongoing.  I am hopeful that authorities will prioritize the testing of essential workers and allow these plants to re-open.  However, this is not a sure thing as many developed countries have failed to take obvious steps to deal with COVID-19 such as hiring contact tracers, setting up quarantine facilities for returning travellers, developing software and IT for contact tracing like South Korea and Taiwan, etc.

The highest quality businesses in this space are:

  • Supermarkets: Costco (COST). Costco stock is a little on the expensive side.
  • Food companies: Tyson (TSN), Hormel (HRL). Tyson is on the cheap side. I am not a specialist in these stocks so I could be wrong about these companies being the higher quality companies compared to their peers. As stated previously, these companies have medium COVID-19 risk depending on how the meat processing situation plays out.

*Disclosure: I am long Tyson and do not understand its industry well.

Auto insurers

Being people aren’t driving, there will be very few car accidents this year. Car insurers will see a brief windfall when its customers work from home and stay at home all day.

Progressive (PGR) and Geico (a small part of BRK.A / BRK.B) are two of the best managed companies in US auto insurance. An investment in Geico’s owner Berkshire Hathaway will have plenty of COVID-19 exposure as Berkshire owns a fairly diverse portfolio of businesses. So, I only consider Progressive to have low COVID-19 risk.

*Disclosure: I own some PGR shares and do not understand its industry well.

Payments companies that do not lend money

Visa (V) and Mastercard (MA) operate payment networks. Their size makes them have inherent advantages over competing payment networks such as Amex (AXP) and Discover (DFS). I’m sure that you are well aware that many of the stores you go to do not accept Amex or Discover. While COVID-19 may reduce overall consumer and business spending, consumers and businesses still need to buy things. There may be a cultural shift towards credit cards over cash as a courtesy to workers who could get infected from handling cash; a few US states encourage restaurants and its customers to pay through credit cards to limit contact. You can sort of think of payments as an essential business because society needs these companies.

V and MA have historically been very good stocks as they have consistently grown their earnings over time.  There has been a slow, long-term shift towards more electronic payments as customers shift to online shopping and use credit cards more frequently for in-person purchases (e.g. rewards, convenience of plastic over cash).  You can find a writeup on Visa at the investing blog titled Mine Safety Disclosures.

Paypal (PYPL) is another good stock although it is currently very expensive.  Paypal consists of multiple parts.  Paypal was originally known for its payment network, used mainly for Ebay transactions.  Because Ebay has been stagnating (and will likely lose market share to Amazon), Paypal’s payment network is not that exciting.  Paypal’s fastest growing segment is its credit card processing business.  For example, I sell software at my website colormancer.com.  Roughly 3% of my sales go to Paypal, which in turn pays its own expenses.  This is a highly profitable business as long as Paypal isn’t getting killed by chargebacks and fraud.  Google Checkout had a very good offering in the same space (I would have used it over Paypal if it were available in Canada) but presumably Google Checkout got killed by fraud and had to stop accepting new customers, putting that business into runoff.  What Paypal is doing is very difficult and they are doing better than Microsoft’s Digital River (I would never use them) and Google.  Paypal does a very small amount of lending ($4B in loans receivable versus a $136B market cap).

*Disclosure: I don’t own V or MA, though it’s possible that I buy both in the future.

Bond rating agencies

My hot take is that bond investors are generally people who want to invest irresponsibly.

The quality of a bond rating isn’t what matters (Institutional Investor has an article on why KBRA has had difficulty in the ratings industry despite quality research).  As long as there is a consensus about a ratings agency’s brand, then the brand has value.  What bond investors actually want are shoddy bond ratings from “reputable” bond rating agencies.  This allows them to appear smart as they buy “safe” investments with a higher yield than other safe investments.  In reality, a lot of fixed income instruments are ridiculously complicated black boxes.  The issuer doesn’t inform investors about what they actually own and nobody understands why the underlying assets need to be sliced into ten million “tranches”.  When loans are securitized, it is critical for the investors to understand loan quality and the underwriting behind those loans.  However, the securitization structure makes it very difficult for investors to understand whether they own good loans or bad loans.  For example, Santander Consumer (SC) modifies delinquent loans and considers them to be current even though the borrower has missed a payment.  This is disclosed in Santander’s 10-K but isn’t explained in the SEC filings for the securitizations.  Santander also doesn’t disclose that it only verifies income on 3% of loans for some of its most recent vintages.

This is the “value” that bond ratings agencies provide. With the rating agency’s stamp of approval, bond investors get to appear to be responsible and prudent investors. (In reality, they invest in black boxes, don’t understand lending, and lack real-world street smarts.) While the subprime housing bubble exposed the industry’s problems, life has gone back to normal and the game has not changed. The bond ratings shenanigans will continue to be highly profitable.

There are only 3 reputable ratings agencies: Moody’s (MCO), S&P (SPGI), and Fitch. The publicly-traded ratings agencies MCO and SPGI are slightly on the expensive side.

*Disclosure: No position in MCO or SPGI.

Cable

The competitiveness of cable varies widely from country to country depending on whether or not regulators grant cable monopolies. In the Netherlands, competition is fierce and the cable company Liberty Global (LBTYA/B/K) has seen its profitability decline there. In the US, cable is more or less a duopoly with phone companies. The duopoly situation in the US is great for profits because the companies can repeatedly raise their prices over time and there is no real competition to get in their way. Historically, the cable and phone companies have not engaged in price wars with one another. As the usefulness of the wires running into the consumer’s home increases (e.g. Netflix and illegal streaming sites are more captivating than cable channels), the US cable companies should be able to slowly raise their prices over time.

Cable companies should do fine in a COVID-19 environment because the Internet is a lot more useful when citizens are at home all day without anything better to do. It also allows employees to work from home and for students to attend their online classes. There may be a short-term speedbump from unemployment as unemployed consumers will likely prioritize rent/savings over cable TV (especially when there are no sports on TV). A short-lived drop in revenues should have very little effect on the stock price.

In the US, Charter (CHTR) is generally considered to have the best management team in the business. Comcast (CMCSA) is another solid cable stock; however, its stock has mostly tracked the S&P 500 over the past five years and has significantly underperformed Charter. Comcast owns many non-cable assets such as cable channels, NBC, etc. Cable channels are a dying business as the world will slowly shift towards Internet streaming and Netflix-like services. Dying businesses have a tendency to underperform the broader stock market so they are “risky” in that sense. I strongly prefer Charter because of its higher growth and very low exposure to dying businesses.

*Disclosure: I own LBRDA, which is sometimes a way of buying CHTR shares at a discount. I do not own GLIBA, which is another John Malone vehicle that owns Charter shares.

American health insurers

American health insurers are incredibly scummy companies that corrupt politicians into creating a grotesque system that delivers shoddy healthcare in a highly profitable manner. If you’re uncomfortable with scumbag companies, then these stocks are not for you.

My post on health insurers goes into why these companies have seen their profits grow at a high rate over the past decade.  Insurers become more profitable with scale because scale gives them negotiating power over doctors and other suppliers.

United Health is the largest and enjoys the most scale advantages. Centene seems to have the best management team as they managed to make money on ACA/Obamacare while their competitors lost money. All of the health insurance stocks have done well historically becausemanagement teams have been open to industry consolidation. If a smaller sub-scale insurer has a low-performing stock, a larger player will happily pay a takeover premium to merge with them.

There is some COVID-19 risk for health insurers although the risks are a little weird and not straightforward. Right now, many people are not getting their elective surgeries for done one of the following reasons:

  1. Their state has banned elective surgeries to free up hospital beds for COVID-19 patients.  (Politicians like Andrew Cuomo and Donald Trump have indicated their desire to have elective surgeries begin again.  Both left-wing and right-wing politicians will be pushing towards restarting elective surgeries once rates of COVID-19 are lower.)
  2. Patients are afraid of going to the hospital because they might catch COVID-19, which can spread rapidly in hospitals.

This has created a very weird situation where hospitals have seen their demand plummet.  A heart surgeon normally performs very expensive surgeries; however, the heart surgeon’s skills are not useful in the battle against COVID-19.  This has destroyed demand for most specialized doctors (and the teams of people who support them).  In turn, this has caused many hospitals to furlough their workers; this is even happening in the parts of New York state that have not been hit hard by COVID-19.  Patients are also avoiding their family doctor so that they can’t catch COVID-19 in the waiting room.  Counterintuitively, COVID-19 has caused healthcare spending to drop even though there is a huge demand for COVID-19 related healthcare.  Insurers will do well as long as they do not have to pay for too many COVID-19 patients.  Public action will play a role in limiting the prevalence of COVID-19 as a portion of the US population will stay home out of fear and ensure that the US stays in a state of partial lockdown.  The current situation may be a short-term boost for health insurers as political inaction and half-baked reopening schemes will ensure that COVID-19 continues to kill and create legitimate fears about going to the doctor or to the hospital.  At the same time, the portion of the American population that is against re-opening will stay home and help prevent the situation from getting completely out of hand.

*Disclosure: Long CNC and UNH.

Technology / software companies

Nothing about COVID-19 prevents people from using software. In some cases, websites like Facebook and apps like Tinder (owned by MTCH) are seeing substantially more usage because people are staying home. In other cases, very few people are using Expedia (EXPE) or Priceline (BKNG) because air travel is down 95%. Obviously, if COVID-19 drags on, the companies that will do well are the ones whose software will see similar or higher usage. While all of the software companies will be affected by consumers and businesses having less money, please keep in mind that recessions are normal and that stocks have gone up during recessions. Stocks tend to crash the hardest when a bubble pops so try to avoid very expensive stocks like Netflix and Amazon.

Facebook and Google are other technology companies whose stock prices aren’t overpriced like Netflix or Amazon. They are market leaders in their niches and are wildly profitable because their websites monetize well through targeted advertising.

Match Group

Match Group’s main asset is the dating app Tinder.  When people are in lockdown, they want socialization because they are getting so little of it.  With a lot of free time on their hands, people are getting their socialization through dating apps.  Instead of buying MTCH directly, it may be a better idea to buy IAC.  IAC is a holding company that owns Match, Angie’s List, Vimeo, Ask.com, and other technology startups like Investopedia.  You essentially get the non-MTCH assets for free.  I have a quick and dirty sum of the parts calculation in this Google spreadsheet (make a copy of it for yourself).

*Disclosure: Long GOOGL and FB. No position in IAC / MTCH.

Verisign

Verisign has a highly profitable monopoly on .com domain registrations.  ICANN determines whether Verisign keeps its monopoly and whether or not the company can raise its prices.  Much to the outrage of domain registrars and businesses, ICANN has opted to continually increase Verisign’s profits.  There is likely some sort of quid pro quo going on between Verisign and ICANN.  Regardless of whether or not that is occuring, it seems likely that Verisign’s profits will grow.

Any recession may reduce Verisign’s profits slightly as part of the domain registration market consists of domain squatters. These companies/individuals buy domain names hoping that a business will pay a very large premium for the domain name. In tough economic times, less of that activity will happen. I don’t believe that any recession-related effects will move Verisign’s stock price much. (Most of the stock’s value is in the next 15-30 years’ cash flow, which will inevitably include some sort of recessionary period in that 15-30 year timeframe.)

*Disclosure: No position in VRSN.

Closing thoughts on risk

If the impact of COVID-19 turns out to be deep and brutal, then almost all assets will have some type of risk associated with them:

  1. Cash will see its value diluted if governments print a lot of money.
  2. Bonds are vulnerable to credit risk and inflation / money printing.
  3. Stocks are volatile due to investor sentiment.  People often overreact and sell them too cheap (or pay too much for them).

My speculation is that there will be a lot of money printing used to prop up banks, lenders, and the wide range of businesses facing bankruptcy. Once people realize what low interest rates and money printing are doing to their wealth, they will likely try to protect themselves by buying stocks (or gold, commodities, etc.). If there is a deep 1930s style depression, there is a good chance that stocks outperform cash though the future is far from certain. My opinion is that 100% cashbonds is not the safest route.

It is also quite unclear as to how COVID-19 will turn out.  There is a huge range of outcomes and I want to believe that the future will be ok.  Science could save us if we take aerosols seriously (*assuming that SARS-Cov-2 behaves like the respiratory coronaviruses that affect livestock).  Technology to deal with aerosols already exists (e.g. the HEPA filters used on airplanes).  While existing vaccines for respiratory coronaviruses don’t deliver lasting immunity, we may invent a new type of vaccine that does.  Scientists may stumble across a meaningful solution such as a highly effective treatment or a highly effective vaccine.  I don’t think that it is a good idea to assume the worst.  The precautionary principle should apply.  If there is a chance that COVID-19 can cause a deep and lasting 1930s-style depression, then we should prepare our portfolios accordingly.  It’s fine to own some of the riskiest stocks as long as you don’t have too much of your portfolio in those stocks.  The systemic COVID-19 risk is fine as long as you aren’t exposed to too much of it.

Stay safe.
