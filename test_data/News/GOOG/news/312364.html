Latest Senate Intel Russia report en route

By TIM STARKS 

04/07/2020 10:00 AM EDT

With help from Eric Geller, Martin Matishak and Alexandra S. Levine

Driving the Day

CHAPTER IV: A NEW TAKE — The Senate Intelligence Committee has unanimously approved the fourth installment of its probe into Russian interference in the 2016 election, Martin reports with our colleague Andrew Desiderio. The move sets the stage for the report — which is undergoing a classification review — and the fifth and final installment that examines whether the Trump campaign colluded with Moscow, to potentially be published before Election Day.

The fourth portion focuses on the intelligence community’s assessmentin January 2017 that the Russian government meddled in the 2016 election with the goal of helping Trump become president — a determination Trump has rejected. The committee “verified” the intelligence community’s conclusion, according to Intelligence member Angus King (I-Maine). “It was an exhaustive review of that report — the sources, the background, everything that went into it and the conclusion was that that report, the original report, which found that in fact there was substantial Russian interference with the [2016] election and that part of the purpose of that interference was to support the election of Donald Trump,” he said. “That has been verified by our committee’s action. … The conclusion is clear.”

The release of the two reports would put an end to the committee’s bipartisan investigation, which began over three years ago. The committee has already released thefirst three chaptersdetailing Russia’s use ofsocial media “troll farms” and cyberattacks on the U.S. political apparatus, including the Democratic National Committee.

EVIL WILL PREVAIL— Registration of coronavirus-related domains, many of them designed for phishing attacks, have risen dramatically, IntSights found in a report out Monday. There were 38,000 domains registered in March that used “corona” or “covid” in their names, up from 5,000 in February, 1,800 in January and 190 in 2019. “Between January and March, coronavirus-themed phishing lures, malware infections, network intrusions, scams, and disinformation campaigns have become rampant across the clear, deep, and dark web,” the company wrote in its report, which offers a broader rundown of all of those phenomena.

IT STILL NEEDS A BETTER NAME — The FBI issued a pair of warnings on Monday about the rise in business email compromise attacks, some centered on the coronavirus and others targeting organizations that use cloud-based email services. An example of the first: “A bank customer was emailed by someone claiming to be one of the customer’s clients in China. The client requested that all invoice payments be changed to a different bank because their regular bank accounts were inaccessible due to ‘Corona Virus audits.’ The victim sent several wires to the new bank account for a significant loss before discovering the fraud.”

BEC attacks on cloud-based email are taking advantageof the fact that while such services have safeguards against BEC, they sometimes have to be enabled and manually configured, the FBI said.

CONGRESS, GETTING THE HANG OF THIS — From our friends at Morning Tech: Beginning today, House lawmakers will now have to email-in bills, amendments and other floor materials — a push to protect members of Congress and their staffers and adhere to public health guidelines during the pandemic.

“Staff must electronically submit all Floor documents — including bills, resolutions, co-sponsors and extensions of remarks — to a dedicated and secure email system, rather than deliver these materials by hand to staff in the Speaker’s Lobby or Cloakrooms,” Speaker Nancy Pelosi announced Monday. “At this time, Members may still drop off materials in person.” These digital procedures will remain in place through at least April 19.

Election Security

STRINGS ON ME— The top Republican on the House Administration Committee on Monday offered his support for amending strings that were attached to the recently appropriated $400 million for coronavirus-induced election expenses. The National Association of Secretaries of State expressed concern about conditions such as requiring states to match the federal funds, spend the money by Dec. 31 and report on the expenses within 20 days of the election. “I write to request that we work together to address the concerns outlined by these election officials, and that we do not implement a federalized approach that will hinder states from successfully executing our elections,” Rep. Rodney Davis (R-Ill.) wrote to Chairwoman Zoe Lofgren (D-Calif.).

LOCK, STOCK, AND BARELY SECURE —The FTC and a Canadian company have reached a settlement over the firm’s alleged deception about the security of its internet-connected locks. Tapplock Inc., claimed that its fingerprint-based locks were “unbreakable,” but as the FTC alleged in a complaint, independent researchers discovered that they had “critical physical and electronic vulnerabilities,” including a Bluetooth issue that could have let anyone unlock nearby locks and an API flaw that could have let hackers steal users’ personal information, including their locks’ locations. “Respondent did not take reasonable measures to secure its locks,” the FTC charged, “or take reasonable precautions or follow industry best practices for protecting consumers’ personal information.”

To settle the charges, Tapplock must undergo biennial security and privacy assessments and must accurately represent its practices from now on. The company’s settlement with the FTC, which the commissioners approved unanimously, is subject to a 30-day public comment period before the commissioners vote on its final ratification.

CHINA VS. SOUTH KOREA —A Chinese cybersecurity firm on Monday accused a hacking group with suspected ties to the South Korean government of launching an extensive cyber espionage campaign against Chinese institutions — but of course, there are doubts about the report. Qihoo 360 said the group, DarkHotel, targeted “Chinese institutions abroad and their relevant government units,” focusing on VPN users.

A researcher from Kaspersky took issue with the report. “This write up is full of speculation, no evidence this was actually [DarkHotel], and a ton of confirmation bias about targeting because of Covid,” tweeted Brian Bartholomew. “Not saying they’re wrong, but in the future, there needs to be more supporting data to support claims.” Qihoo 360 has previously alleged DarkHotel attacks on China, and last month accused CIA hackers of waging an 11-year espionage campaign against the Chinese government in a report that also encountered criticism over its attribution.

That said, DarkHotel has had its share of attention lately, too. Google last month reported on hackers who used zero-day vulnerabilities to target North Korea, and Kaspersky said the activity was linked to DarkHotel.

People on the Move

— Trump’s pick to serve as CIA inspector general, whose nomination he sent to the Senate on Monday, will bring some cybersecurity experience to the job. Peter Thomson, a Louisiana attorney, is co-chair of Stone Pigman Walther Wittmann’s information security practice.

—Sue Gordon, former principal deputy director of national intelligence, joined CACI International’s board of directors, the company announced Monday.

Report Watch

—Security pros are seeing a rise in cyberattacks since the coronavirus outbreak, with 71 percent reporting an increase, according to a survey out today from Check Point and Dimensional Research. They said phishing attacks were the leading threat. What’s more, 95 percent said they were facing new IT security challenges during the pandemic, with secure remote access for employees topping their list.
