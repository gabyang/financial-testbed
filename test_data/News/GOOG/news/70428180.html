The Rise Of Streaming Video Brings New TV Programming Gatekeepers

Now, even the most renowned entertainment companies with vast libraries of content such as AT&T's HBO unit are kneeling before streamers to get their content in front of audiences. (©irina_levitskaya — stock.adobe.com)

Licensing
  • PATRICK SEITZ
  • 08:00 AM ET 09/09/2021

Not so long ago, traditional pay-TV services controlled what programs people got to watch, as a handful of major cable and satellite providers oversaw the distribution of content. But times have changed. Now, the rise of streaming video has created new gatekeepers for what we see on television.

Instead of companies like Comcast (CMCSA) and AT&T's (T) DirecTV ruling the roost, streamers like Roku (ROKU) and Amazon's (AMZN) Fire TV are the new kids on the block, supplanting cable and telecom services. The change has altered the power dynamic in the television industry. It's also made the programming game a bit more cutthroat in the process.

At one time, television networks bowed before cable services to get their material on the air. Lesser-known networks took what they could get. Meanwhile, powerful players such as ESPN were in a better position to negotiate terms.

Now, even the most renowned entertainment companies with vast libraries of content such as AT&T's HBO unit are kneeling before streamers to get their content in front of audiences, and they're not always successful.

The transition is so dramatic that even longtime cable television mogul John Malone admits the game has changed.

"I think these global platforms will be enormously powerful," Malone said in an interview with CNBC last November. Roku, Amazon and other streaming platforms have a reach that U.S. cable TV firms just can't match, the Liberty Media chairman said.

Creating Bottlenecks For Services

The shift has helped the shares of Wall Street newcomer Roku soar. Meanwhile, already mighty Amazon gets even more valuable and other streamers see their fortunes rise. It's also empowered consumers longing to watch what they want when they want.

That is, if their streaming platform isn't playing hardball with content providers.

With millions of users of their devices, streaming video platforms can dictate terms to content providers. Instead of being neutral, the streaming platforms won't carry channels unless they get a cut of their revenue. That's led to some channels being blocked on streaming platforms, including smart TVs and set-top boxes.

AT&T's HBO Max service struggled after it launched in May 2020 because it wasn't on the Amazon and Roku platforms for the first six months.

AT&T Chief Executive John Stankey criticized the streaming services for their hard-nosed negotiations.

"Where the bottlenecks are sometimes occurring are in these commercial agreements," Stankey said at the Wall Street Journal's annual Tech Live event last October. "We should ask ourselves, is that friction somebody really feeling their oats and maybe having market power above and beyond what's reasonable for innovation?"

Streaming Video Brings New Bosses

Further, anyone who thought programming blackouts common with traditional services would end with streaming video was wrong.

For instance, on Aug. 17, Roku settled a carriage dispute with Charter Communications (CHTR) and agreed to carry Charter's Spectrum TV app again. The dispute started last December when Roku pulled the Spectrum app from its download store.

"It's 'meet the new boss, same as the old boss,'" said Phillip Swan, editor of TVAnswerman.com, quoting The Who classic tune "Won't Get Fooled Again."

Programming blackouts on various streaming platforms are likely to crop up from time to time as contracts come up for renewal, he said.

"It shouldn't surprise anyone because of the economics involved," Swann said. "It's just cable and satellite 2.0."

Consumers Find Workarounds To Blackouts

The situation is frustrating for consumers who can't get all the streaming services they want on their platform of choice.

For instance, streaming platform LG Electronics on Aug. 3 finally announced a deal to carry the HBO Max app, more than 14 months after its launch.

Christopher Majewski, a Cincinnati-based artist, cheered the long-awaited news. He told Investor's Business Daily he was using a workaround to watch HBO Max on his LG smart TV.

"I didn't enjoy turning on an additional device (game console) simply to access HBO Max for the months I had to," he said via email. "Having the app and others like it on my LG OLED is energy efficient and convenient, and for me, convenience is key."

Isaac Rouse, a writer and editor in New York, used Apple's (AAPL) Airplay wireless app from his iPhone as his workaround to get HBO Max on his LG TV. But the quality wasn't great, he said.

"It was extremely frustrating, especially since it (HBO Max) is becoming one of my favorite streaming services," Rouse told IBD.

Streaming Video Platform Conflicts

Such streaming blackouts aren't uncommon. Amazon didn't agree to carry Comcast's Peacock service on its devices until late June, nearly a year after its launch. HBO Max didn't get its app on Vizio (VZIO) smart televisions until a deal was reached on Sept. 1.

And Roku still has an ongoing dispute with Google over the search giant's YouTube TV app.

Roku is the top streaming video platform in the U.S., according to Parks Associates. Samsung's Tizen-based smart TVs are the second-largest streaming platform in the U.S. They're followed by Amazon Fire TV and LG's WebOS-based smart TVs, Parks said. A different study, though, shows Samsung as the worldwide leader. (See chart.)

The top three streaming video platforms account for 65% of the viewing in U.S. broadband households, Parks said.

"Those are the real power players now," Parks Associates analyst Paul Erickson told IBD. "The landscape has shifted."

Parks Associates says 10 major streaming video platforms are vying for the gatekeeper role on U.S. televisions. Others include Alphabet's (GOOGL) Google TV, Apple TV and Vizio.

Comcast Eyes Smart TV Play

Consumer behavior spurred the shift to new television gatekeepers, Erickson said. Last year, streaming became the most common way for Americans to consume video, he said. Streaming services like Netflix (NFLX) and Walt Disney's (DIS) Disney+ reported big increases in subscribers during the Covid-19 pandemic as consumers stayed home.

Meanwhile, traditional pay-TV services are bleeding subscribers as consumers turn to streaming services over broadband internet connections.

In the second quarter this year, traditional pay-TV providers in the U.S. lost 1.29 million subscribers. It ended the period with about 70.7 million subscribers. At the end of 2018, those companies had over 85 million subscribers, according to Leichtman Research Group.

Cable TV giant Comcast is looking to do a late-cycle pivot to catch up to the streaming video leaders. It plans to introduce a smart TV later this year that would work with any broadband service, the Wall Street Journal reported. That would allow Comcast to compete with Amazon and Roku beyond its own network.

Comcast has been working with retailer Walmart (WMT) and Chinese TV manufacturer Hisense on the smart TV, the Journal said.

But Roku, Amazon and Google plan to keep up the pressure on traditional TV services.

Roku, Amazon, Google Push Cheap Gear

Roku is even willing to lose money on its streaming devices to continue to add new viewers to its platform. It lost money on its players in the second quarter and expects the losses to continue for the rest of 2021. Roku makes most of its money selling advertising on its platform, which boasts 55.1 million monthly active accounts.

Amazon has been aggressively pushing its Fire TV streaming video devices, including low-cost streaming sticks and televisions. Amazon said its Fire TV streaming sticks and Fire TVs from Toshiba and Best Buy (BBY) brand Insignia were huge sellers during its Prime Day promotion in June.

Google also has been ramping up its effort to get its software on more smart televisions. It is working with smart TV maker TCL, a longtime Roku partner, on a lineup of Google TV sets. Google also sells inexpensive Chromecast streaming devices.

Streaming Video Platforms Can Promote Services

Getting a video service onto a streaming platform is just the basic level of support, Parks analyst Erickson said. Given the explosion of streaming services, content providers often must ink deals for premium placement of their shows on the platform's home screen so their service isn't buried under rows of choices.

Apple recently cut a deal with Roku to get its Apple TV+ service on a button on the Roku remote for quick, one-touch access.

"Getting people to the content that they want to watch faster is key to keeping them engaged," Erickson said. "And constant engagement is a key to keeping them subscribed to your service."
