House Democrats’ bill bans price-gouging on items related to Covid-19

By ALEXANDRA S. LEVINE 

04/08/2020 10:00 AM EDT

Presented by

With help from Cristiano Lima, Leah Nylen, Steven Overly, Sam Mintz and Mark Scott

Tech of the Town

HOUSE DEMS TEE UP COVID-19 ANTI-PRICE GOUGING BILL —House Democratic leaders on Tuesday introduced a bill that would ban price gouging for items related to the coronavirus outbreak, according to a copy of the text obtained by POLITICO. (Nope, the practice is not already illegal at the federal level.) The COVID–19 Price Gouging Prevention Act, unveiled during a pro forma session by Reps. Jan Schakowsky of Illinois, Frank Pallone of New Jersey, David Cicilline of Rhode Island and Jerry Nadler of New York, among others, would empower the FTC and state attorneys general to fine sellers who use the emergency to “increase prices unreasonably.”

— Remember: Sens. Amy Klobuchar (D-Minn.) and Thom Tillis (R-N.C.) also introduced two bills recently that would let the FTC tackle price-gouging during emergencies, as we reported in MT.

— State enforcers have sounded off on the continued prevalence of price-gouging scams across major online marketplaces,including Amazon, eBay and Walmart, calling on the e-commerce giants to crack down on the trend. House Democrats last month included language expanding regulators’ powers to punish price gougers in a massive coronavirus relief proposal, but those provisions did not ultimately make it into Congress’ $2 trillion bipartisan measure. The bill notably does not call for penalties for online platforms that fail to curb such items.

MEANWHILE: EBAY CRACKS DOWN ON SNEAKY PRICE-GOUGING WORKAROUND — To bypass eBay’s price-gouging rules, sellers have been listing a cheap item — MT found listings peddling pennies and nickels — that include a “free gift” of Lysol disinfectant spray. Some of those listings sold for between $37 and $99. But in a move to better target price-gougers, the online auction site will now remove listings that offer free disinfectant or hand sanitizer perks, a spokesperson told MT.

— eBay said it has a zero-tolerance policy on price-gouging, and as of last Friday, the platform said it had blocked or removed more than 5 million listings. After an inquiry from MT, eBay said it would update its automated sweeps and blocking tools to address these kinds of attempts to exploit policy loopholes.

TRUMP RECYCLES AN ATTACK ON AMAZON — Trump’s coronavirus briefings have featured praise lately for the tech industry’s assistance, but on Tuesday he revived an accusation we haven’t heard in a while: Amazon is killing the U.S. Postal Service. Trump told reporters that USPS should charge the e-commerce giant and other internet companies more for delivering their packages — asserting that “they lose money every time they deliver a package for Amazon or these other internet companies.”

— In fact, package deliveries have been a rare source of revenue growth for the Postal Service.And while Amazon, like other bulk shippers, negotiates a special shipping rate with the USPS, the agency cannot legally strike a deal that causes it to lose money. However, the Trump administration suggested the agency charge higher rates in a 2018 report.

— Trump’s latest attack comes amid questions about whether the Postal Service is receiving enough financial aid to weather the coronavirus crisis. Trump said Tuesday that Democrats could “truly save the post office” if they focused on Amazon and other e-commerce firms.

A message from CTIA:

SUPERCOMPUTING CONSORTIUM HOMES IN ON TARGETS —The federal labs, corporations and universities that have harnessed their collective supercomputing power to study the novel coronavirus have started work on 11 research projects, organizers said Tuesday.

— The COVID-19 High Performance Computing Consortium, an initiative announced by the White House and IBM last month, has received 35 proposals to date from researchers whose work could be conducted more quickly with the help of ultra-fast computers. Those proposals focus on studying the protein structure of the virus; identifying sites where molecules can bind to coronavirus cells; and forecasting the spread of the virus.

— Researchers believe their work could lead to new treatments for coronavirus, or a better understanding of why some patients are more prone to severe symptoms. “It’s a race against time — in our aim to defeat the deadly coronavirus that has so far infected over a million people and killed tens of thousands,” IBM’s Dario Gil and the Energy Department’s Paul Dabbar, who co-chair the consortium, wrote in a blog post. Other members of the consortium include Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Nvidia, Hewlett Packard Enterprise and the National Science Foundation.

UPDATE ON THIS WEEK’S PAPER HEARING —Quite a lengthy list of witnesses is on tap for Thursday’s congressional “paper hearing” on how consumer data is being used to fight the spread of the pandemic and the privacy concerns that may arise. The Senate Commerce Committee updated the witness list on Tuesday — we’re now expecting testimony from the Center for Democracy and Technology; Future of Privacy Forum; ACT | The App Association; Interactive Advertising Bureau; Network Advertising Initiative; Kinsa Smart Thermometers; and the University of Washington’s law department.

— Meanwhile: “White House senior adviser Jared Kushner’s task force has reached out to a range of health technology companies about creating a national coronavirus surveillance system to give the government a near real-time view of where patients are seeking treatment and for what, and whether hospitals can accommodate them,” POLITICO’s Adam Cancryn reports. The effort would “represent a significant expansion of government use of individual patient data, forcing a new reckoning over privacy limits amid a national crisis.”

NEW ROADS FOR NURO —The driverless delivery company, which recently received a first-of-its-kind exemption from federal regulators, also got permission from California’s government on Tuesday to test its vehicles on public roads with no drivers behind the wheel. The approval, which applies to nine cities, is only the second time California has taken that step, with the first having gone to Waymo. In a blog post, Nuro said it will start the delivery service to select customers in the Mountain View area, with plans to expand.

DISSECTING COVID-19 FAKE NEWS —Researchers at the Reuters Institute at the University of Oxford have delved into the early trends of coronavirus misinformation, and our Morning Tech colleagues in Europe got hold of an early copy of their findings.

— Interesting: Almost two-thirds of the fake contentis either derived from situations taken out of context, manipulated or given a different meaning than was intended. To the researchers, that demonstrates that what most people are seeing, when it comes to Covid-19 misinformation, isn’t some elaborate, state-backed campaign, but rather people doing what they sometimes do when faced with a crisis: Make things up while looking for answers.

— So who’s behind most of this misinformation?The academics broke it down into two categories. Those at the top (politicians, celebrities, dare we say journalists?) represented 20 percent of all the misinformation posted on social media, but constituted 69 percent of all social engagements (shares, likes, etc.) on the digital platforms. That means if a relatively small number of people were more circumspect with their views online (we’re looking at you, 5G-denying celebrities), it could make a big difference to how such fakery is spread online. Kudos to Finland.

— More from the Oxford study:“More than half of the misinformation about the coronavirus pandemic that has been debunked by fact checkers remains on Twitter without any warning label,” WaPo reports, “a record that puts it far behind rival social media platforms.”

A message from CTIA:

5G is the fastest growing generation of wireless and Americans all over the country are using it to create the future and enhance America’s economic competitiveness. 5G is connecting kids to learning opportunities, fighting wildfires, making farms more sustainable, manufacturing smart, healthcare more accessible, driverless cars a reality, and bringing much needed competition and choice to home broadband. Thanks to these innovations, in the past two years demand for 5G data nearly doubled with experts projecting it will triple by 2027. But while demand continues to grow, the supply of spectrum to meet that demand stayed flat. Studies show we will need more than 400 MHz in the next three years to secure reliable 5G for all. To expand access, drive innovation, grow our economy and create jobs, America needs more 5G spectrum. Learn more at CTIA.org.

About The Author: Alexandra S. Levine

Alexandra S. Levine is a reporter covering the intersection of technology, government and public policy, and she is the author of POLITICO’s popular daily newsletter, Morning Tech. She is focused on the crosscurrents between Silicon Valley and Washington D.C., and how technology is shaping the 2020 presidential election.

Prior to joining POLITICO in 2019, Alexandra was a Metro reporter at The New York Times, where she spent three years at the helm of the New York Today column and contributed enterprise stories to the National, Travel and Style sections.

She earned a master’s degree from the Columbia University Graduate School of Journalism and a bachelor of arts in English from the University of Pennsylvania. She is also an alumna of the Fellowships at Auschwitz for the Study of Professional Ethics, a program in Germany and Poland that explores the ethics of reporting on politics, war and genocide.

When not on the tech beat, Alexandra can be found doing improv, training for her next marathon and ghostwriting wedding toasts under the pseudonym, “The Toastess.”
