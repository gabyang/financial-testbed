Facebook, other apps have been so bad, but they can do so much good | Opinion

  • Published: Apr. 12, 2020, 1:13 p.m.
By
  • Star-Ledger Guest Columnist

By Linda Stamato

Internet companies, as the world’s dominant information publishers, have profound social responsibility. They account for a large share of our nation’s economy as well. And, right now, they, including Facebook, Google and Twitter, among others, are showing their good side. They are intercepting and removing misinformation and ‘fake news’ relating to the coronavirus, for example, marking posts and videos that promote conspiracy theories as false and directing visitors to reliable sites, such as the CDC.

Just recently, Facebook, Twitter and YouTube cracked down on the exaggerated claims for a certain drug treatment being propagated by Fox News “hosts” and others, including Rudy Giuliani and Brazilian President Jair Bolsonaro, hardly trusted voices, who are touting the drug’s efficacy.

The bad of social media is well known, of course. It’s on the internet platforms, after all, where rampant abuse, disinformation -- falsehoods deliberately intended to mislead -- hate speech, lies and threats appear, are conceived, delivered and amplified by anonymous voices that post, forward, tweet and re-tweet to maximize the damage.

And, then, of course, there are the well-documented, and continuing efforts by Russia to use social media to influence our elections, to cause disunity by intensifying suspicions about “the other,” and, lately, to ‘share’ false information about the disease to spread chaos. Variations on the Russian playbook are being adopted by political disinformation purveyors on American soil as well in the hope of influencing elections.

At the same time, there is much good that social media is doing, not only in removing and correcting and re-directing posts in order to thwart “fake news” about the virus but to provide accurate information, and, to make connections among those who can help one another, including using platforms to share ways for people to help themselves -- and others -- cope with the impact of the disease on their communities.

The good that is happening gets far less play and yet it’s becoming increasingly valuable as the nation faces a pandemic of proportions we have yet to fully grasp.

Consider the example of Nextdoor, a private, “neighborhood” app where every user is tied to a physical address so fake accounts are essentially nonexistent. It’s increasingly popular, with its virtual communities covering more than 180,000 neighborhoods in the U.S. More than 90% of those, moreover, are in America’s 25 largest cities.

In Morristown, social media is not only raising awareness of the potential impact of COVID-19 on Morristown Medical Center, but folks connecting on the sites are doing something about it. In fact, no sooner had word reached Morristown that the hospital might come under siege from the spreading virus, at least two efforts were launched to demonstrate to the hospital’s staff what ‘community’ means.

In the first instance, under the auspices of NextDoor, a group is providing food, in individually wrapped snack packages, to the Emergency Room staff at the hospital, for two shifts a day. It’s contemplating expanding to provide crocs (the rubber footwear the staff wear and easily wear out) as the hospital requests them. The effort is likely to keep on going so long as the funds come in and the need is there.

Others are using Facebook. This group of Morristown residents is working together to help make sure dozens of elderly residents don’t go hungry. Roughly 75 elderly Morristown residents have had their meal programs suspended and are now facing food insecurity.

“Because we’re in this together,” wrote organizers on Facebook. "It’s our duty to pick up the slack!” And so it organized its “Morristown Order Out Twice a Week Stimulus Plan,” and is currently signing up restaurants in town to commit to donating one lunch for each upcoming week. One visit to the site suggests just how well the local business community is responding!

In Nextdoor, and similar sites and pages, we may be seeing models for meaningful, positive social media.

Local, relevant, reliable and secure communication on social media can provide structure to a functioning society as well. By inviting active, direct involvement by citizens, NextDoor, Facebook and other apps may help to support sound knowledge about public health and other matters, even as, by enhancing community connections, they solidify the base of democracy that those who abuse social media are determined to undermine.

Linda Stamato is co-director of the Center for Negotiation and Conflict Resolution at the Edward J. Bloustein School of Planning and Public Policy at Rutgers University in New Brunswick, and a faculty fellow there.
