Adeluge of digital information from smartphones and other sources is pulsing through the global economy. Forward-thinking companies from tractor makers to restaurant chains are harnessing this data in astonishing new ways to build better products. They are solving problems that once seemed unsolvable, and they are reinvigorating mundane businesses with clarity and precision. Savvy investors should take note.



Take Callaway Golf’s new line of Mavrik drivers. The sleek clubs bear little resemblance to the cookie cutter clubs of the past, for good reason. Mavrik wasn’t designed with human eyes. Callaway tapped a supercomputer and artificial intelligence software to shape the metal in ways that surprised veteran designers.

That might seem like overkill, but designing golf clubs puts physics to the test. You have to get the aerodynamics right to increase clubhead speed. The face of the driver needs to provide the right combination of rigidity and deflection. The goal, according to Allan Hocknell, Callaway’s senior vice president of research and development, is to have the face reliably deform so more energy gets transferred to the ball at the moment of impact.



Normally, driver designs will go through eight to ten iterations to solve this complex problem. Mavrik underwent thousands. For four weeks, 24 hours per day, seven days per week, a supercomputer crunched through algorithms and simulations. After each iteration, the computer made subtle changes and diligently moved on. In the end, the club face it designed was noticeably thinner in the middle, but the biggest difference was on the backside. While normal clubface backs are smoother than the greens at Augusta, Mavrik features swirls and seemingly random lines. Some parts are thin, other parts are thick. Hocknell says “the computer stretched our way of thinking” about the research and design of golf clubs.

Data and new digital tools helped solve a design problem that would have been impossible if company engineers were left to traditional devices.



Better problem solving is also playing out at McDonald’s. The fast food chain makes 70% of its sales from customers who prefer not to get out of the car. The problem is the company has no idea what the customer is going to order when they pull up. A complicated choice can back up the whole line. To speed things up, and increase sales, McDonald’s executives began working with Dynamic Yield, a company that makes personalization software based on artificial intelligence.

The code allows McDonald’s to dynamically change drive-through order menus. Selecting a Big Mac might automatically suggest a combo package, or an Oreo McFlurry if it’s hot outside. During peak hours, hard-to-prepare items like scrambled eggs might get eliminated entirely. Shrinking drive-through lines comes with the added benefit of influencing what customers order, in real-time, across the entire network of stores. You think you have free will, but software is manipulating your choices.



It’s part of a larger digital plan. The company began placing order kiosks in its stores in 2017. The new point-of-sale system connects with profiles stored inside the McDonald’s mobile app. Customers can keep favorites, track past orders and store payment options. They also get to skip the line by ordering directly. The result is a more predictable sales, more loyal customers and a new business model based solely only understanding data.



It’s the same premise behind the spectacular growth at Mastercard and Fair Isaac & Co. While neither company is probably top of mind when it comes to digital transformation, both are pioneers in the movement. For two decades, Mastercard executives have been tirelessly working to kill cash, the connective tissue of commerce in the physical world. And Fair Isaac, best known for its FICO scores, was integral to the growth of credit cards.

Bill Fair and Earl Isaac were ahead of their time. The pair met at Stanford University in the early 1950s. Fair was a brilliant engineer. Isaac was a gifted mathematician. They combined those skills to build a revolutionary credit scoring system in 1956.

It seems staid now but at the time it was a radical idea. They were using math, computers and predictive analytics to determine consumer credit risk. By 1972 the company had automated the credit score processing system. A decade later, Fair Isaac automated the credit account management systems, too. Faster processing led to explosive growth in credit cards issuance.

Sixty years later, credit is proving to be a very lucrative business for Fair Isaac. Today, FICO scores are used in some 90% of all lending decisions. All of the 100 largest U.S. credit card issuers, and 95 of the largest 100 domestic financial institutions are clients. Fair Isaac works with 600 global insurers, and more than 400 retailers and general merchandisers. In 2018, its Falcon Fraud protected 2.5 billion credit cards.

Many of those cards share Mastercard branding. The company is often misunderstood. Investors assume its business is the issuance and management of its ubiquitous credit cards. That’s false. The Purchase, New York, company is a payments processor. It sits between the merchant’s banks that collect payments, and financial institutions that issue its cards. For this service, the company earns a small but persistent fee. It’s a wonderful business with one driving theme: Make all payments digital.

Keeping this in mind, Mastercard executives are fortifying their network to protect merchants and banks. Ed McLaughlin, president of operations and technology, says the company is using artificial intelligence to look at every transaction in real time. Often the software detects fraud patterns that would have gone undetected by previous rules-based systems. Another network system uses biometrics to determine if the person making the transaction is the cardholder. It analyzes whether the digital transaction is being made using a computer or a smartphone, which hand is being used and the typing cadence. It’s security at the system level, and the stakes are high.



Within a decade, Mastercard projects that machines will begin transacting digital payments. As many as 40 billion devices will be connected. Gas pumps and carwashes will hook up with the computers in cars. And everything will share a connection with encrypted, personal financial information stored in the cloud. Given its scale and expertise, Mastercard is a natural gatekeeper. It’s a massive opportunity, forged with data.

Artisans and engineers have been forging copper for centuries. Now Rio Tinto PLC, the giant mining company, is willing to go to great lengths to get it. Its new mine near Superior, Ariz., bores nearly 7,000 feet below the Earth’s surface. There, temperatures routinely hit 175 degrees. Warm water falls from overhead rocks like rain. The 1.3-mile-deep shaft is being excavated by Resolution Copper Mining, a joint venture with BHP Billiton Ltd.

It’s a project no sane executive would have green-lighted a decade ago. The technical challenges boggle the imagination. The attraction is the opportunity to change the business of mining with more sensors, autonomous vehicles and data analytics.



Caterpillar Inc. is building custom electric loaders, excavators and other robotic gear for the project. They will be equipped with thousands of sensors to achieve full automation. Its distinctive yellow machines will find the ore, mine it and transport it to the surface, all under the watchful eye of technicians hundreds of miles away. If everything goes according to plan, humans will be kept largely out of harm’s way. Resolution will be a productivity game changer—as well as a digital transformation avatar.

Caterpillar began research and development of automated vehicles in the 1980s. The first commercial self-driving trucks for mining were deployed in 2013. Since then the fleet has grown to 275, operational at seven large mining companies. Caterpillar’s Command fleet has hauled 1.9 billion tons of iron ore, copper and oil sands with no lost time due to injuries. The added benefit of autonomy is that fewer people are roaming around the job site.

That’s a good thing. The new Cat 797F is 31 feet wide and stands 25 feet tall. The tires alone are twice the size of a large SUV. The hulking dump truck is capable of payloads of 363 tons. That’s about five and a half times heavier than the space shuttle. It’s a unique tool built to solve an unfathomable problem.

Golf clubs, behind-the-scenes financial instruments and autonomous mining equipment are only a few of the ways smart companies are using digital transformation strategies to impact everyday businesses. The applications are wildly different, but the impact is the same. The best companies are using data analytics and artificial intelligence to deliver better products to customers, faster.

For a long time, product development was essentially a high stakes game of hit and miss. Companies came up with good ideas, they ran focus groups and prototype teams. They put together marketing squads to gussy up the end product. Then they sold it, or tried to.

Digital transformation tools are removing a good portion of hit and miss. Mavrik has been a huge winner for Calloway Golf. McDonald’s solved its drive-through problem. Fair Isaac and Mastercard are delivering billions of dollars in value-added services to their clients. And Caterpillar is building products to automate one of the world’s most dangerous occupations. These companies are using new digital processes to optimize their businesses.

Digital transformation is a breakthrough that dwarfs earlier business developments like assembly lines and mass-market advertising—and is just getting started. Investors need to realize that users of artificial intelligence, networking and sensors are going to generate at least as much new wealth as the technology makers who usually drive the conversation.
