Do Certifications And Accreditations Help Reduce Ad Fraud?

Do Certifications and Accreditations Help Reduce Ad Fraud? Simple answer: no. But you didn’t click on this article to read a one word answer. So here’s the longer explanation of what certifications and accreditations do, and don’t do, when it comes to fighting digital ad fraud.


What Are Certifications and Accreditations?

Let’s start by understanding what certifications and accreditations are using two specific examples from the digital advertising industry: TAG Certification and MRC Accreditation.

TAG stands for Trustworthy Accountability Group. TAG was created in 2016 and funded by industry trade associations - IAB (Interactive Advertising Bureau), ANA (Association of National Advertisers), 4As (American Association of Advertising Agencies), and agencies like GroupM and other ad tech companies sit on their Board and Leadership Council. At a high level, the TAG certification process includes, but is not limited to, 1) completing paperwork where the applicant declares various company practices around fraud, 2) creating a position called TAG Compliance Officer, and 3) paying the annual fees. This is what they call “self-attested” certifications. In some cases, applicants go through additional steps, as witnessed by a third party, and get certified with “independent validation.” Companies buying ads from TAG certified vendors can reasonably expect that the “inventory has been filtered at 3+ points during the transaction” according to a TAG spokesperson.

MRC stands for Media Ratings Council. The MRC is a standards setting-body for digital advertising. Standards created by the MRC, such as viewability and IVT (invalid traffic), provide uniform definitions and measurement details so data collected by different parties can be compared apples to apples — i.e. they are measuring the same things in the same ways. Additionally, the MRC provides accreditations to companies after a rigorous review process, in which applicants provide a list of things they measure, and the accounting firm PwC meticulously verifies they are indeed measuring what they said they would measure. If they are doing so, they become accredited by the MRC.

What They Do and Don’t Do

Most marketers assume that certifications and accreditations help reduce fraud. They don’t. But they can be helpful in the following ways. Buying from TAG Certified vendors means these companies have at least declared that they will do various ad fraud related checks before selling the ad inventory to you. Just be aware that fraudsters will say they do those checks, but actually never do them. Accredited vendors are also better than non-accredited ones because at least a third party, PwC, which is known for its auditing capabilities, looked under the hood. But note the nuance - MRC accredits companies for measuring what they said they would measure, not for measuring ad fraud correctly. In order to do that, any provider of accreditations would have had to have their own technology to collect data, their own truth sets to differentiate bots versus humans, and analytical experience to interpret the data correctly. That’s really hard. So the MRC accredits companies for measuring what they said they would measure, not for measuring ad fraud correctly.

Certifications and accreditations DON’T mean ad fraud is measured correctly or even that fraud is low.

Bad guys are also known to be on their best behavior while going through the certification process and then start the fraud later. It’s well documented there are fraudulent and malicious apps in Google Play. How did they get there? They got through a review process that checked for malicious code. But what if the malicious code was injected after they got approved? And there’s even a documented case where a company bought up real apps that were struggling. And then use them to commit ad fraud by pumping more volume through them using bots.

Once fraudulent companies get certified or accredited, they start their fraudulent activities. With certification, they can operate in broad daylight. Their sales materials and press releases tout the fact that they are “TAG Certified Against Fraud.” On the flip side, honest companies that were not out to commit fraud, don’t use malicious code to cheat, and have low bots on their sites (e.g. good publishers) don’t need certification in the first place. Advertisers that buy media from them will already get what they were expecting - ads shown to human audiences, no shenanigans.

So the moral of the story is certifications and accreditations are good to have. They are like a first line defense against basic ad fraud and unscrupulous practices. They are also guidelines and guardrails. But bad guys don’t follow guidelines and they bust through guardrails and other defenses.

As long as you, the marketer, are aware of how cunning bad guys can still commit fraud even while certified and accredited, and keep a vigilant eye out for yourself, your digital marketing campaigns will be fine.Those certifications and accreditations were not protecting you from ad fraud, YOU were.
