Cure For A Boring Zoom Party: Hire A Comedian, Magician Or Petting Zoo

Abram Brown
Former Staff
May 1, 2020,06:30am EDT
Updated May 1, 2020, 11:17am EDT

In their 20-year careers, Doug McKenzie and Ryan Oakes, a pair of professional magicians, have made many things disappear: cell phones, doves, watches, silk handkerchiefs, rings, dollar bills, computers, people. Then in March something else vanished quite unexpectedly. Their entire business.



﻿They typically perform at least 100 times a year. Sometimes separately, sometimes together as a duoknown as Digital Deception—their routine partly relies on smartphones and other devices—at corporate events for $15,000 to $20,000 a pop, annually adding up to well over $1 million. But those lucrative gigs were all swiftly cancelled as COVID-19 swept across the world, leaving McKenzie, 39 and Oakes, 41, retreating to their Brooklyn apartments and hash out a plan.

Within days, they emerged with an outline of “Alakazoom,” a 20- to 45-minute,virtual version of their actdone, yes, through Zoom’s video-conference app. They’ve since mounted around 30 performances, including ones for a Google team (where the group’s leader picked up the tab), a Westchester County, New York country club and “a private family on a yacht in Miami,” McKenzie says. The price for these virtual shows has usually been up to a third of their former rate.



“There is digital conferencing fatigue. We’re trying to provide a service that at least lightens that fatigue,” says McKenzie. “Our show is interactive, and right now, what we’re missing is human contact. We’re trying to bring that—really make an experience that’s not like you could be watching YouTube and see the same thing.”

We’re all pretty tired of seeing the same things, doing the same things—stuck as we are at home living out the same daily rhythms. Everyone is a bit strung out. And a laugh these days can seemas scarce asfive-ply Charmin.

At the outset of lockdown, our calendars filled up withvirtual happy hoursand simultaneous Netflix viewings. Seven weeks later, those ideas have gotten stale, and digital gatherings are quickly evolving again. Increasingly, gig entertainers are being hired to perform at virtual parties often arranged by coworkers or groups of friends. “I found it very entertaining,” says Michael Bolognino, the Google executive who arranged for Digital Deception to amuse his 12-person team. “The best part for me was my team having this gift of an entertaining end to a Friday.” People do like to spend money on this sort of thing. Americans spent an average of more than $1,400 hosting and planning parties, according to a 2019 pollof 2,000 people completed for BJ’s Wholesale Club, though those numbers will surely decrease during this wheezing economy.



Magicians are only one genre of virtual performer. With the world ill at ease, comedians are especially popular. “I’ve performed for people in California, in Oregon, in New York,” says Dewayne Hill,a Tampa, Florida, funnyman. “It’s cool that I’m able to do it and don’t have to leave home.” Other top acts include mind readers, illusionists, jugglers and ventriloquists like Atlanta’sWillie Brown. “People love it—you don’t get a chance to see a ventriloquist that often,” says Brown, 57. “And it’s just like TV coming right at you.”

Another big category: barnyard animals. Over Easter weekend, Grant Phillips, the 31-year-old owner of Squeals on Wheels,aMaryland-based traveling petting zoo, completed more than two dozen 15-minute virtual Easter bunny visits for $30 each. For these events, the star was a sweet-natured Flemish giant rabbit named Poppy, who is the size of a small dog. (She was well compensated with strawberries for her time.)



More recently, Free the Facts, a Washington, D.C., democracy-education non-profit, hired Phillips to dial into a Zoom call where the group presented prizes to employees—Greatest of All Time, or GOAT, awards for which Phillips’ Nubian goat Carmela was on hand to present the commendation. And then the National Association of Convenience Stores two weeks ago bought Phillips’ “Hoppy Hour” package ($60), where he did a Zoom presentation that featured his rabbits, llamas and pigs. It “gave our employees with children a chance to connect with animals on the same computers they see us working on all day,” says Greg Levitan, the association’s HR executive who arranged the “Hoppy Hour.” “It also reiterated one thing that we think is important: There is a huge difference between being isolated and being alone.”

“People love seeing and learning about [the animals]. On a lot of the Zoom meetings, people take pictures of everyone on the call—plus, like, a hedgehog,” says Phillips. “Incidentally, I’m just in the background of a million pictures. I don’t know how many pictures there are online of me holding the animals.”

Even with all these gigs, the economics for Phillips are dire, as it is for many other entertainers. Their old business models are shattered. Phillips has laid off his three full-time employees and his 15 part-timers, but he is still scrambling to come up with the few thousand dollars a month to feed his animals. (The largest animals, the llamas, are the most expensive, eating through $40 a week in bales of hay.) His $60 fees represent only a fifth of what he used to be able to charge, and he is fortunate that he has no rent: The animals live on property owned by his parents, Squeals on Wheels’ original founders.



Hill, the comedian in Tampa, is finding times tough, too. Pre-pandemic, Hill, 41, stuck mostly to the corporate event circuit, charging $2,500 to $3,000 an outing. “And they would hand me the check after the show happily,” he says. In the new marketplace, Hill has been getting only $300, but now it’s more likely executives, not their employers, paying the bill. That was the case recently for shows he did for teams at Berkshire Hathaway HomeServices (the real estate arm of Warren Buffett’s conglomerate) and Royal Bank of Canada. “They wanted me to come in and just break up the monotony of them sitting at their computer screens,” Hill says.

With live events unlikely to return in any form for many months, these entertainers are rushing to rewrite portions of existing acts that can’t work virtually while continuing to develop original, quarantine-ready material. “It’s everyday finding time to tweak, develop new materials so that if people are asking me to come back and do repeat events, they will see a completely different performance,” says Colin Cloud, 33,a mindreaderwho, until COVID-19, had a standing residency at The Mirage in Las Vegas. When Cloud does dream up fresh stuff, he workshops it through Facebook Live.



They are also shortening their routines. Hill has cut his down by a quarter to less than 40 minutes in his case—to account for shortened, at-home attention spans. And he has begged audiences to break digital etiquette by unmuting their mics to help recreate some semblance of a live show. He still does dress the same: red button-down, black leather vest, gigantic Rolex Yacht-Master. “I want them to give the same feel as if they were live. It’s my responsibility as a professional entertainer.”

Jon Wee and Owen Morse,two highly daring jugglersknown as The Passing Zone, have actually gotten the chance to add something back to their routines. “There are so many venues these days that don’t allow us to use fire, but we can use fire in our own space,” says Wee, who gets together with Morse at their Orange County studio to broadcast over Zoom. “We do a thing where we juggle a flaming toilet plunger, a stun gun and a rat trap.”



“I was talking to Penn the other day—of Penn and Teller—and he goes, You know this changes everything, right?” recalls Morse. “I don’t think we’re gonna be on a stage for another 12 months or something.”

Empty theaters and altered economics are forcing another big change for the entertainers: They will need to do more less lucrative performances to make up for lost income. Without having to travel, there’s a chance that they can start doing multiple performances per day, though the demand for virtual gigs hasn’t yet reached this level.

“We’re willing to work cheaper because we don’t have to get on an airplane and travel around the country and have the headaches of staying in hotels and lugging our stuff,” says Wee. “The nice thing is,” says Morse, “clients won’t have to pay for add-ons, like shipping our chainsaws.”
