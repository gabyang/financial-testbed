The secret stories behind some of Hull's most famous pubs

From grisly murders to exotic zoo animals and kayaks to vicars, these Hull pubs hide fascinating histories

    Hull has some fantastic pubs.

    Steeped in history and always there when you need an ale and a catch-up with friends, there are few places in the country that can boast the quality of boozers we have.

    There has been a renaissance in the popularity of Hull’s pubs over the past decade, bringing historic buildings back to life and buzzing with conversation as they would have been centuries ago.

    They also have some very interesting back stories, which means you have even more to talk about next time you're nursing a pint in a nestled corner.

    From grisly murders to exotic zoo animals and kayaks to vicars, here are just some of the stories behind Hull's most historic pubs.

    The Punch Hotel, Carr Lane

    The ornate pub dates back to the mid 1800s, with the first landlord recorded as William Livingston in 1846, when the Punch opened up onto Waterhouse Lane.

    William Livingston remained at the Punch Hotel until April 1848, after he failed to keep up with mortgage payments and was subsequently forced to leave. He went on to become landlord of the Bull Inn in Stepney.

    When William Livingston was landlord, inquests into the deaths of Hull folk were held at The Punch.

    The first took place on Thursday, April 22, 1847, and concerned the death of a man named Henry Shaw.

    Mr Shaw was a gardener, employed by a Mr Briggs of Wakefield. He was in ill health and was visiting Hull, staying at the Punch Hotel to rest.

    On the Wednesday evening he was feeling much better, and visited the Amphitheatre, but later that night when returning to the hotel felt ill. Staff found him upstairs in his room groaning and a surgeon was sent for but he died before he arrived.

    The official verdict returned was “Died by the Visitation of God.”

    The hotel went through a dark patch in the 1880s and was linked to an infamous Hull murder in March 1886.

    It was revealed James Storey, a regular drinker at the Punch Hotel, was called as a witness in a murder trial.

    It was claimed that William Corp, a 49-year-old tailor, of 6 John’s Place, off Edward’s Place, Hull, had murdered Louisa Wharam. Mr Corp and Mr Wharam had rowed at the pub and Wharam ended up dead.

    Hull Cheese, Paragon Street

    Formerly named the Paragon Hotel, Hull Cheese earned its name from an old saying to describe someone who was drunk.

    If someone says to you, “You have eaten some Hull Cheese”, they are essentially saying you are drunk, and with Hull famous for its strong ales, the name was aptly chosen for the establishment.

    More than many other places, the Hull Cheese, which was built in the early 18th century, has its fair share of tragedies according to local historian Mike Covell.

    Among the dark and devious crimes committed here was one by Maria Moody, described as a "dangerous" and "violent woman", who assaulted George Ringer with a broken glass, before then attacking the arresting officer PC Pearcy, in November 1889.

    In June 1890, Henry Emsley Midgley was attacked in the billiard room by Louis Hickman over a dispute.

    Manchester Arms, Scale Lane

    Built in the late 1700s, The Manchester Arms is named after the HMS Manchester, which is believed to have been the first ship to enter Hull’s dock.

    However, it had several names before 1876, including The Slaw Bone and The Earl Grey.

    A mystery death at this particular pub spooked the city when Margaret Goy suffered a serious injury to her head on her way out of the building in August 1926.

    Her husband, who had left her for five minutes, was informed she had been taken toHull Royal Infirmary when he returned but, within 24 hours, she was dead.

    At her inquest, some of the eyewitnesses claimed she fell, while others said she was the victim of a serious assault. The result was an "open verdict", with the case attracting nationwide media attention.

    Minerva, Nelson Street

    A haunt for dockers, the small, snug rooms of the historic pub are still open for drinkers to sit in while looking out on the Humber Estuary.

    However, the pub has not been short of tragedy over its almost 200-year lifespan, as Mike Covell states.

    In the 1830s, not long after it was built, this pub suffered its first tragedy when Jane Cortis, wife of landlord Richard, died there. She was just 43 years old and left a husband and ten children, two of which were only six weeks old.

    Historic crimes committed in Hull

    Its proximity to the waterfront meant that it would be close-hand to any seafaring tragedies, including when the steam packet, Gainsboro, exploded in the Humber Dock in 1837, killing 17 men.

    It was later reported that one of the bodies had to be recovered from the roof of the Minerva.

    Parkers, Anlaby Road

    A popular watering hole for football and rugby fans, Parkers has been renamed several times over the past few years.

    An exact date for when it opened isn’t clear, but the pub quickly became associated with death due to the many inquests it held in the 19th century, when it served as a Coroner’s Court.

    Even the landlord, Tom W Halliday, couldn't escape the grim reaper when his body was discovered by his wife at the hotel in August 1876, aged just 33.

    Halliday was well known, well loved, and well respected in Hull, and was up for election to be a member of the Newington Local Board.

    The inquest lasted days before a verdict of natural causes was returned.

    Whittington and Cat, Commercial Road

    The Whittington and Cat is very much a family-run pub, but also served as a Coroner’s Court more than 100 years ago.

    Originally taken over by Samuel Wood in 1808, he passed it onto his son Anthony in 1841.

    Tragedy struck the family, though, when Anthony's sister, Maria, died at the pub when she was only 40 years old.

    In 1864, Anthony Wood walked into the back room of the public house, which housed the brew house, and took his life.

    He would be later found hanged in what is now the rear bar, and was just 51 years of age.

    A jury returned a verdict of "temporary insanity".

    Ye Olde White Harte, Silver Street

    Originally built as a private residence, the pub is reported to be one of the most haunted in Hull, and surrounded by mystery.

    So many stories have been told about plots, skulls, and antiquarian discoveries that somewhere along the way we appear to have lost track of the history and the people who lived, worked and died there.

    Inside The Plotting Room restaurant in Ye Olde White Harte in Hull
    View gallery

    One of the more infamous cases covers the tragic death of Fanny Clarkson, 26, who was daughter of the then landlord, John Clarkson.

    Miss Clarkson was tasked with lighting the fire in the main bar in April 1806 but, when she went to light it, the flames set fire to her clothing, causing fatal burns.

    The next tragedy took place in April 1912, when staff turned up for work but found the doors locked.

    The pub's cellar man William Strutt, found the landlady Clara Seapelt, aged 38, dead at the bottom of the stairs.

    She had suffered problems with her breathing and had passed out at the top of the stairs before falling down and breaking her neck.

    Visitors should go and see the mysterious skull on display, and have a meal in the upstairs Plotting Room where spooky guests will no doubt join in.

    The Lion & Key, High Street

    The Lion & Key started out as a public house in 1812, when the Britannia Coffee House stood on the site.

    Shortly after it was renamed The Lion & Key to commemorate Wellington’s capture of Ciudad Rodrigo in the Peninsular War, which ended in 1814.

    The pub closed and was turned into offices, but was given a new lease of life in the 1980s when it reopened as a boozer under the name Durty Nelly’s, when a live Irish band would perform for the customers.

    In the early 2000s, it was renamed Lion & Key.

    King William, Market Place

    Rumour has it when Hull Minster’s bells toll midnight, King Billy dismounts from his golden horse and pops into the nearby pub for a pint or two before the sun comes up.

    Originally a coffee house, the pub dates back to the 1700s, and over the years has been a shop selling all manner of wares, until becoming the pub we know today in the 1860s.

    Ye Olde Blue Bell, Market Place

    A lot of the pub was demolished during the expansion of Hull’s Trinity Market in the 1960s, but The Blue Bell is still a popular watering hole in the Old Town.

    The pub is believed to have been a popular coaching house from the late 1700s, as a pit-stop for carts travelling around Hull and East Yorkshire.

    The Bonny Boat, Trinity House Lane

    Serving since the late 1700s, but under the name The Bank Hotel, The Bonny Boat is believed to have got its name from the kayak of an Inuit who died while being brought to Hull from Greenland in the early 1600s.

    The name change came about in the 1840s, and there is talk the “bonny boat” is in reference to the Inuit’s kayak which can still be seen in Hull.

    Nightlife nostalgia

    One theory suggested that Queen Victoria came to Hull and, upon seeing the kayak, said: “That’s a bonny boat.”

    But this was debunked when it transpired the Royal’s visit was in 1854 – at least 14 years after the pub changed its name.

    The Mission, Posterngate

    The former chapel can still be seen as drinkers find themselves seated in pews and surrounded by stained glass windows.

    The chapel was founded in the 1920s for seamen in Hull, and from the 1880s it was a club and a hostel for sailors in the Local Marine Board Offices opposite.

    The Polar Bear, Spring Bank

    First opening in the 1840s, the pub was named after the popular polar bear pit at the nearby Zoological Gardens, in the current Hutt and Peel Streets, which was open from 1840 to 1861 and housed other animals including wolves, elephants, tigers and pelicans. 

    At that time, the pub was situated further down Spring Bank, on the corner of Carlton Terrace, and moved to its current location in 1860.

    The pub has a ceramic bar similar to that found the The White Hart, in Alfred Gelder Street, of which only 10 survive in England.

    The Empress, Alfred Gelder Street

    It was originally named The Dockside Tavern, as Queens Gardens as we know it today was once a huge dock.

    The building itself was a former dock warehouse, but soon became a pub with the mounting passing trade from seamen and merchants.

    It is believed the name was changed to The Empress in the late 1800s, to signify Queen Victoria’s role as the Empress of India.

    Atom Brewery Co, North Church Side

    The Old Corn Exchange, now Atom Brewing Co, was originally called the Excise Coffee House and, in 1788, was leased from the Corporation by John Meggitt, a local brewer, for £15 per annum.

    This is when the Corn Exchange name was applied.

    The pub was once also known as Percy's Palace, after licensee Percy Johnson.

    Sailmaker’s Arms, High Street

    The pub takes its name from the 19th century ships’ chandlers, Rayment and Sharp, who originally had the premises.

    An advert for the company shows they were ship chandlers, bonded store dealers and sail makers at the premises, supplying equipment for ships and boats.

    The White Hart, Alfred Gelder Street

    Not to be confused with the older Ye Olde White Harte in Silver Street, this pub was built in 1904 and was frequented by Philip Larkin, as well as many people working in the city’s Old Town at the time.

    It houses one of the UK’s only listed bars, which was built at the same time as the pub by men who also worked on the Titanic.

    There are rumours a passage under the cellar of the pub leads directly into Ye Olde White Harte, with staff members the first to admit to hearing bumps in the night.

    Three John Scotts, Lowgate

    According to current owners Wetherspoon, the church opposite the pub, St Mary’s, was run for most of the 19th century by three successive vicars – all called John Scott.

    The pub itself was a former general post office, which stands on the site of Suffolk Palace, named after the De La Pole family of Hull who were the Earls of Suffolk.

    Suffolk Palace was built during the 1380s and occupied much of the area between Lowgate, the Dock and the Land of Green Ginger.

    George Hotel, Land Of Green Ginger

    The hotel that never was.

    The former gate house to the long-gone, original George Hotel, this pub dates back to the 17th century and is apparently haunted by several spectres, including the Grey Lady who has a habit of roaming the pub at night.

    Visitors walking past will be able to see England’s smallest window, where it is believed a young boy would sit identifying hotel guests and allowing them entry.

    The now-demolished hotel would have been a welcome sight for the merchants and seamen mooring at the newly opened Queens Dock in 1778.
