Agile Stars Testify Today Before Congress

The four big tech companies—Amazon, Apple, Facebook and Google— are due to testify today before the Antitrust Subcommittee of the U.S. Congress.

These four firms have achieved unprecedented wealth in a short period of time. Together they are worth more $4 trillion, or roughly equivalent to a fifth of the entire U.S. economy. The firms argue in their opening statements that they have made their money legitimately as a result of the extraordinary ongoing innovation.

Why These Firms Are So Valuable

What are these market-leading firms doing that makes them so much more valuable than any other firms in history? The first thing to grasp is that this is the wrong question. These firms are not just doing things differently. These firms are thinking differently. They have embraced radically different principles from those of the top 20th Century corporations. The result is a once-in-a-century event: a phase change, or turning point, in leadership and management. These firms have redefined the very concepts of leadership and management for the 21st Century.

Although each of the market-leading firms has its own idiosyncratic strengths and flaws, by and large they share a common set of principles with a distinctive ideology, architecture, and dynamic for getting things done. The terminology, systems, processes and practices of the firms vary considerably, but the underlying principles have a strong family resemblance.

First, the ideology: these firms are obsessed with delivering value to those human beings who use the firm’s products and services;profits are the result, not the purpose of the firm.

Second, the architecture: work is organized to draw on the full talents of those doing the work, often through small self-organizing teams, working in short cycles.

And third, the dynamic: the firm operates as far as possible as a horizontal network of competencerather than a vertical hierarchy of authority.Leadership and innovation are expected from everyone.

Wall Street has recognized the difference in these new firms and has rewarded them with stratospheric valuations.

Four Different Businesses

The five top-market-leading firms are often lumped together as “Big Tech” as though their success—and what sets them apart—is the result of technology. Today, all companies rely on technology and yet not all firms are so successful. Lumping them together hides the fact they’re very separate and distinct—not just as companies, but in terms of their business models and practices.

·       Alphabet’s Google helps users search for things and gets paid for accompanying ads if searchers click on them; their ads reach customers exactly at the moment they are looking for a product.

·       Amazon has multiple businesses and keeps inventing more. It began as a retailer—"the everything store”—has moved on to manufacturing, as well as software services from which it currently makes most of its money.

·       Apple is a hardware technology company, deriving most of its revenue from the iPhone, iPad and Mac computers.

·       Facebook operates like a traditional ad-supported media company, providing audience-generated content to a vast global audience.

Calling these four firms “Big Tech” is apt in the sense that they are certainly big and, like all firms today, they use technology. But “tech” doesn’t explain their extraordinary success or what sets them apart. Former market-leaders of the 20th Century like GE and IBM also invested heavily in the same digital technology and data, which are now largely commodities.

These Firms Have Benefited Their Customers

The hearing today is an antitrust hearing. Much of the discussion about the companies has focused on the fact that they have become so big and powerful. But being big and powerful in itself is not a violation of antitrust law or crime.

One problem for the legislators is that antitrust violations have traditionally depended on the customer being harmed.

Big Tech will argue that customers have benefited enormously from their activities.

  • Google has massively expanded access to knowledge
  • Facebook has enhanced communication and community
  • Amazon has made shopping cheaper and faster and better
  • Apple’s multi-functional iPhone has changed our lives in multiple ways.

So the firms will argue that by definition, on the current law of antitrust, they cannot be guilty of antitrust violations.

The question for the lawmakers is whether the law should be changed.

Possible Areas Of Wrongdoing

Lawmakers may argue these firms have abused their market power in various way and suggest that anti trust law should be rewritten to cover damage to other businesses.

For instance, the arguments that lawmakers may explore include the following.

Google

  • That Google rigs online search results to benefit Google’s own products.
  • That Google participates in its ad auctions as both auctioneer and participant.

Facebook

  • That Facebook functions as a media company and should be subject to the same responsibilities of other media companies with liability for misstatements.
  • That Facebook abused its market power in its acquisitions of Instagram and WhatsApp, forestalling the competition that they would have offered.

Amazon

  • That Amazon uses Amazon Marketplace and AWS to learn about other businesses and then enter into unfair competition with them.

Apple

  • That Apple uses its market power to unfairly favor its own music services over those of competitors like Spotify.
  • That Apple unfairly charges 30% fees to customers wanting to access firms like Spotify.

A Test For Lawmakers

In his opening statement, Jeff Bezos noted that when he was trying to raise money for his fledgling business 20 years ago, he was often asked by potential financiers, “What is the Internet?”

The hearings thus represent a test for lawmakers as well as the CEOs. In previous hearings, lawmakers often appeared befuddled and lacking a fundamental understanding of the digital economy. It will be enlightening to see what happens today.
