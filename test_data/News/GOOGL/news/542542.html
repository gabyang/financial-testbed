With help from Steven Overly, John Hendel and Leah Nylen

Driving the Day

FIRST THINGS FIRST: TRUMP’S LATE NIGHT BOMBSHELL —President Donald Trump announced on Twitter late Monday that he will “temporarily suspend immigration into the United States” due to the coronavirus pandemic. The move is expected to be closely watched by leaders in Silicon Valley, who have criticized the administration’s immigration policies and whose companies rely on workers from overseas.

SOCIAL MEDIA CAUGHT IN THE MIDDLE OF COVID FEUDS —Social media companies’ handling of coronavirus-related content is increasingly becoming a point of partisan contention, as politicians, protesters and other actors push the boundaries of what’s permissible on major platforms during the pandemic.

— The latest flare-ups:Facebook’s decision to block anti-lockdown protesters from organizing on the social network in some states with stay-at-home orders triggered instant criticism from Republicans and plaudits from one Democrat. “Now, more than ever, companies like Facebook should focus on connecting people, not shutting down communities because they hold different views,” Sen. Ted Cruz (R-Texas) told POLITICO. Democratic Sen. Richard Blumenthal (Conn.), meanwhile, said that “Facebook is right to take a stand against harmful misinformation.” As Steven reports in a dispatch, the dustup presents “the latest no-win quandary for Facebook.”

— And it’s far from the first Covid feud for Silicon Valley:Facebook has separately taken flak from at least one Trump ally, Sen. Marsha Blackburn (R-Tenn.), for surfacing information from the World Health Organization, which the president has attacked for its handling of the virus. Twitter, meanwhile, has taken heat from liberals in recent days over its decision not to take enforcement action against tweets in which the president said it was time to “LIBERATE” states under lockdown orders. And it’s drawn cheers and jeers from opposite sides of the aisle for cracking down on Trump surrogates spreading coronavirus misinformation.

Q&A: SILICON VALLEY’S CONGRESSMAN ON BIDEN’S TECH AGENDA — Khanna (D-Calif.) endorsed Biden for the presidency on Monday, adding to a growing list of prominent progressives to back the presumptive Democratic nominee. Your host caught up with Khanna after the announcement to ask him what a Biden presidency would mean for the online industry, and what he makes of the former veep’s agenda on tech policy so far.

— Biden would be an ally in growing digital jobs in rural areas beyond Silicon Valley, said Khanna, who has made that one of his signature causes. “I do believe he understands the importance of extending the technology revolution to places left out,” Khanna said during a phone interview.

— Biden can win over Sanders voters by leaning into his views on labor. “One of the places that I think the vice president actually is very good is [his] labor platform about proper classification of employees and employees’ collective bargaining rights, making sure workers can have good working conditions and I think emphasizing that labor platform would go a long way,” he said. (Khanna was Sanders’ national campaign co-chair, and just last month touted the Vermont senator’s sweeping victory over Biden in Silicon Valley districts in California’s Democratic primary.)

— Biden has yet to outline a comprehensive online privacy plan, but Khanna said he expects a potential Biden administration to follow in the footsteps of former President Barack Obama, whose White House released a so-called privacy bill of rights as early as 2012. “I think they will be consistent given that President Obama tried to have an internet bill of rights, a privacy bill of rights that didn’t get traction — enough traction, but I think the time now is more receptive,” Khanna said.

FIRST IN MT:HOUSE BILL WOULD REQUIRE WARNING LABELS FOR TIKTOK, FACEAPP — A House bill set to be introduced today would require consumers to click through a warning before downloading software or an app originating from countries deemed a national security risk, including China and Russia.

— It’s the latest congressional measure to target popular apps like the Russia-based FaceApp and TikTok, owned by the Beijing-based ByteDance, over privacy and security concerns. The standalone Online Consumer Protection Act, slated to be unveiled during today’s pro forma session by Rep. Jim Banks (R-Ind.), would empower regulators to level civil and criminal penalties against companies that fail to display the notice.

— “Parents and consumers have a right to a warning that by downloading some apps like Russia’s FaceApp or China’s TikTok, their data may be used against the United States by an adversarial or enemy regime,” Banks, a member of the House Armed Services Committee, told MT. Sen. Rick Scott (R-Fla.) last year introduced a separate bill to require app distributors to disclose the origin of products on their online clearinghouses, citing growing concerns over the security of TikTok and FaceApp.

A message from CTIA:

HOW LOW CAN IT GO? — Google’s lobbying spend, that is. The search giant shelled out just $1.8 million to lobby the federal government in the first quarter, a staggeringly small sum for a company that until recently was among Washington’s biggest spenders. In fact, Google has not posted a lobbying total that small in any single quarter since 2011. (For comparison, Google spent $3.36 million in the first quarter of 2019.) The company’s D.C. office got new leadership in 2018 and its lobbying payments have been on the decline ever since.

— Amazon, on the other hand, had another record-high quarter.The e-commerce giant spent $4.33 million to lobby the federal government in the first three months of the year. As for the other tech giants: Apple shelled out $2.16 million and Microsoft ponied up $2.39 million; Facebook’s report had not posted before MT tucked into bed.

WHY THE FCC DOESN’T WANT TO VET TRUMP’S COVID BROADCASTS —FCC general counsel Tom Johnson joined The Federalist on Monday to discuss why the commission quickly shot down a recent emergency petition from advocacy group Free Press asking the agency to investigate what it calls bogus coronavirus information from talk radio and White House task force briefings. (Free Press’ request cites a 1992 commission rule prohibiting the knowing broadcast of information that could cause public harm.)

— One key reason for declining the request:The FCC has often botched its judgments about communications networks over the decades, Johnson said. “If we often get it wrong even in the core area of our expertise, imagine us trying to make judgments about the results of clinical trials for potential treatments for Covid while those things are still ongoing,” he said. “It would be an absolute nightmare.”

— He called the attempt to get the FCC to probe coronavirus rhetoric on talk radio a “wolf in sheep’s clothing” for reviving the Fairness Doctrine (a repealed U.S. communications policy that required broadcasters to give equal time to different ideological viewpoints).

ROBOCALLS DECLINE AFTER VOIP SERVICE TARGETED — An effort by federal agencies last month to target internet-enabled telephone services that facilitate illegal robocalls has led to a drop in nuisance calls. Andrew Smith, the head of the FTC’s Bureau of Consumer Protection, said Monday that efforts by his agency and the FCC aimed at Voice over Internet Protocol service providers are having an effect.

— “When we have taken actions against VoIP service providers, we’ve actually seen measurable drops in robocall volume,” Smith said during a virtual event hosted by the American Bar Association. “What we have seen over the last several weeks is a pretty dramatic drop in robocalls.”

— Recent enforcement: In late March, the FTC sent warning letters to nine VoIP service providers involved in robocalls. A week later, the FTC and FCC jointly warned three other VoIP providers and urged U.S. Telecom to take additional steps to block robocallers. In March, the FTC received more than 181,500 complaints about robocalls, according to the agency’s Consumer Sentinel database. In March 2019, that number was about 551,500.

A message from CTIA:

5G is the fastest growing generation of wireless and Americans all over the country are using it to create the future and enhance America’s economic competitiveness. 5G is connecting kids to learning opportunities, fighting wildfires, making farms more sustainable, manufacturing smart, healthcare more accessible, driverless cars a reality, and bringing much needed competition and choice to home broadband. Thanks to these innovations, in the past two years demand for 5G data nearly doubled with experts projecting it will triple by 2027. But while demand continues to grow, the supply of spectrum to meet that demand stayed flat. Studies show we will need more than 400 MHz in the next three years to secure reliable 5G for all. To expand access, drive innovation, grow our economy and create jobs, America needs more 5G spectrum. Learn more at CTIA.org.
