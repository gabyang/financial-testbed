Many U.S. government agencies are running on less reliable, less feature-rich fortressed versions of commercial clouds. With a more modern approach to the cloud, governments can gain the agility that today’s most resilient businesses use to serve changing customer and employee needs while keeping data protected.

Modern information technology fundamentally improves how governments are able to interact with their citizens. It enables elected leaders and other officials to communicate with constituents in new ways, reduces the friction of applying for permitting and benefits, and makes it easier to govern remotely when in-person meetings just aren’t possible.

Federal leaders past and present have recognized these opportunities and have worked hard to establish a foundation upon which agencies can build their digital transformation initiatives. I’m proud of my work with many of these leaders in my previous government roles, where our focus was on modernizing federal IT.

But now is the time to accelerate our efforts. The COVID-19 pandemic has made it clear that government operations need to run on more modern technology, and they need to make the transition more quickly. As federal and state governments sent their employees home, capacity and accessibility demands quickly overwhelmed IT systems and processes. Many agencies struggled to provide services using newer digital channels. And, unfortunately, new security challenges have emerged as well, particularly among agencies with services hosted in specialized “government clouds” that have strained under the load.

We believe there’s a better way. It starts with acknowledging that there really is no need for specialized government clouds that give the illusion of a digital fortress. With people doing work from so many locations and on so many devices, there is no longer a firm network perimeter inside which all essential IT interactions occur. This means that techniques focused on standing up and defending a perimeter are like attempts to fortify a fortress’s walls when many valuable items are outside. It also assumes no one inside the fortress is a threat, which is questionable because insider threats—whether malicious or unintentional—constitute a large percentage of data breaches.

Because they are built in this way, government clouds have actually hindered the federal government’s ability to take full advantage of the security and product capabilities of commercial cloud environments. In this article, we’ll look at how this happened, and what the technology industry is doing to provide better options for government customers.

Government clouds vs. commercial clouds

The idea of a government cloud was introduced in 2013, following the introduction of the Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP), which sought to standardize security assessment, authorization, and continuous monitoring for cloud products and services throughout the U.S. government. To meet new FedRAMP security requirements, many cloud providers built separate environments to run government workloads. While this enabled them to more easily achieve compliance, the government clouds didn’t (and still don’t) come with all the benefits that a commercial cloud provides. And their shortcomings have been magnified in today’s “new normal.”

A cloud built like an isolated fortress is rigid and not optimized for constant modernization.

New features are introduced daily into commercial clouds. But because government clouds are run in specialized, stand-alone data centers, they often endure lags before receiving new features. This greatly impacts a government’s access to critical new technologies, whether it’s data analytics, artificial intelligence and machine learning, or even new security protections. A cloud built like an isolated fortress is rigid and not optimized for constant modernization. Now that the concept of a network perimeter has waned in importance, architecting government clouds in this way provides neither the security it once did nor the agility that agencies need.

Embracing “zero trust”

As mentioned, to satisfy FedRAMP standards, government clouds have adhered to antiquated, perimeter-based security models that were the norm nearly a decade ago. However, in today’s modern work environment—exacerbated by the recent pandemic—we know that the goal should not be to encase digital interactions within a protected fortress. Rather, it should be to manage and protect each individual interaction, regardless of where it occurs or originates from. This is because employing a perimeter model generally assumes every interaction inside the perimeter is safe—which given recent hacks is not necessarily the case, and may leave organizations overly vulnerable to insider risk.

Nearly 10 years ago, Google decided that every employee should be able to work securely from any network without using a VPN. This was not an easy decision, but it has drastically shifted the way Google—and the technology industry overall—thinks about ensuring security for distributed workers. We called our effort BeyondCorp, and the concepts that drove it have evolved into a larger industry movement dubbed “zero trust.” A zero trust model does not assume that being on or off the corporate network makes you more or less trustworthy. Each access decision considers the user’s IP address, their device, their behavior, the sensitivity of the resource, and a host of other factors. Zero trust removes the explicit requirement of building a perimeter because it doesn’t automatically assume users are trusted even when they’re inside. 

Getting more with Assured Workloads

This notion of zero trust laid the foundation for a new way of thinking about government clouds. For most major cloud providers, the only option to offer services that are compliant with U.S. government standards is to create a separate government cloud for any government content. While this helps meet requirements for data residency and administrative access, it has also led to fewer data center locations and siloed infrastructure that delivers a more limited set of services. As a result, government clouds do not offer the advantages of a full commercial cloud.

Cloud vendors have been working diligently to address these shortcomings. For example, Assured Workloads for Government—Google Cloud’s solution to support government compliance needs—overcomes these limitations and provides the same services and benefits that organizations in other industries enjoy, without sacrificing security or compliance. 

With Assured Workloads for Government, Google Cloud customers can quickly and easily create controlled environments where U.S. data location and personnel access controls are automatically enforced in any of our U.S. cloud regions. This service simplifies the compliance configuration process and provides seamless platform compatibility between government and enterprise environments. Assured Workloads for Government helps government customers, suppliers, and contractors to meet federal government security and compliance standards, while still having access to all the latest features in our portfolio.

These capabilities are complemented by Google Cloud’s Confidential Computing capabilities. Google Cloud encrypts data at rest and in transit, but customer data must be decrypted for processing. Confidential VM instances offer memory encryption so that customers can further isolate and protect their workloads in the cloud. Google Cloud is the first major cloud provider to offer this level of security and isolation while giving customers an easy-to-use solution that doesn’t require changing code in apps or compromising on performance. 

IT agility is vital to an organization’s ability to respond to crisis, and with a more modern approach to the cloud, governments can gain the agility that today’s most resilient businesses use to serve changing customer and employee needs while keeping data protected. Effective cloud services are always beholden to moving targets, and thanks to modern cloud technologies, governments can increasingly achieve the same flexibility enjoyed by cutting-edge organizations—an important step in responding not only to today’s ongoing challenges but also to whatever rapid shifts in goals or needs emerge next.
