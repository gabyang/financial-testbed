An open analysis of Police Violence

From individual accounts to national statistics

Amid the ongoing protests and calls to action to fight systemic racial injustice, I have felt that often confusion and dispute regarding the statistics about disproportionate police violence and other symptoms of racial bias continue to prevent people from acknowledging, let alone tackling the issue. While many analysts more experienced than myself have published statistics on police violence, it is often challenging to understand the full process of going from raw data to careful interpretations of statistical results. I have tried here to detail my analysis of police violence from start to finish (for full detail, all code used is available here), and I hope that the results can help in understanding the gravity of the current situation.

As a disclaimer, my training is in bioinformatics and not sociology, so there are likely adjustments and corrections I have not included that would be necessary for formal academic work in this field. However, I have attempted to tackle this problem as best I can with my understanding of statistics and the data I could find. While the exact numbers I computed here may not be perfect, I believe that they capture some of the overall trends and severity of racial bias with regards to police violence.

Datasets

I had previously heard that much of the raw data regarding police violence tended to be incomplete, inaccessible, and/or disorganized, and after a few hours on Google this appeared to be the case. However, one particular source, Mapping Police Violence (MPV), did an excellent job of painstakingly recording all publicly known police killings since 2013, along with original sources and extensive metadata such as victim age/race and location. MPV has already done a great deal of analysis and presentation of their data on the national and state level. However, with the amount of extra detail they provided, it seemed possible to analyze this data at a finer level, taking into account the demographic and economic background associated with individual incidents. In my attempt to address this, I downloaded the most recent Census population estimates by race and USDA median income estimates for individual counties (2018 estimates). The former data could allow for estimates of racial bias to be calibrated against local demographic backgrounds, while the latter could act as a proxy for understanding how affluent or poor those locations are.

Defining Bias in Police Violence

With the data at hand, I was interested in quantifying police bias that might be defined informally as “the increase in probability that the victim of a police killing is of a given race, relative to the White population and taking into account the local population distribution by race.” This is unfortunately the best definition I could come up with without getting into some math.

Estimating bias at a national level

I first attempted to estimate the racial bias terms at the national level, i.e. looking at all incidents recorded by MPV with detailed enough location data to be mapped to individual counties. This yielded 3357 White, 1928 Black
and 1331 Hispanic cases. I also included the 117 Asian cases with sufficient location data, which is likely too few to give a precise estimate of racial bias regarding Asians (and consequently excluded from following subgroup analyses), but may give a starting point for evaluating some of the privilege conferred by existing notions of “model minority”.

These results are tragically in-line with the national-level statistics that MPV has published. My estimates for the relative increase in risk of police violence for Black and Hispanic people compared to White people were of 3.4 and 1.7 respectively, similar to the 2.6 and 1.5 relative increases in police homicide rates reported on the MPV website. However, also surprising is the extraordinarily low relative risk of 0.4 for Asians, i.e. an Asian person is less than half as likely to be involved in police violence than a White person and over 8 times less likely than a Black person.

Bias vs. Location Median Income

I next tried seeing how these bias terms change when grouping the incidents by the median income of the county in which the they occurred:

To my surprise, this showed that the relative risk of a Black person being a victim of police violence increases steadily with the overall wealth of a location (as inferred from median income), and only drops off slightly for the richest areas. Particularly, in areas where the median income exceeds $60000, Black people are more than 4x more likely to be victims of police violence than White people, even worse than the 3x increase in risk at the national level as estimated both here and by other sources including MPV. The trend is less apparent for Hispanic people, but the results nonetheless show that racial bias in police violence exists across areas of all income levels and especially in richer areas.

Bias vs. Victim Age

Similarly, I also tried seeing how the bias terms change when the incidents are grouped by the victim’s age:

Again, stratifying the data reveals how much worse the extent of bias can be for specific demographic groups. The already high relative risks of police violence for people of color are more than doubled when examining just victims under the age of 20 (Black: 3.4 -> 8.5, Hispanic: 1.7 -> 3.6) and remains substantially elevated when considering victims under the age of 30. Only above a victim age of 30 is the risk of police violence comparable to the national all-ages estimate of bias, and only above the age of 50 does racial bias become negligible.

Bias vs. Geography (Rural, Urban, Suburban)

Finally, I tried grouping the incidents by location geography based on MPV metadata:

Grouping the data this way again highlights how much more severe the relative risk of police violence is for people of color in specific regions. Urban areas suffer from double the nation-wide risk of police violence for Black people (3.4 -> 7.0), and more than 50% higher for Hispanic people (1.7 -> 2.7). Suburban regions experience similar levels of race-specific risk compared to nation-wide estimates and Rural regions experience the smallest differences in risk, but nonetheless all three types experience some level elevated risk for police violence against people of color.

Conclusions

Altogether, the MPV dataset paints of sobering picture of the prevalence and severity of racial bias in incidents of police violence. The increased risk for people of color exists everywhere from the poorest to the richest counties, across rural, suburban, and urban communities, and directly impacts people of all but the oldest age groups. Nationally, Black and Hispanic Americans experience a 3.4x and 1.7x increase in risk of police violence compared to White Americans, respectively. However, these risks, particularly against Black Americans, are further increased for specific demographics and locations, such as victims under 20 (8.5x), in counties with median income >60k (~4.0x), or in urban settings (7.0x).

I hope that this analysis, though brief, provides a more transparent look into understanding quantitatively the biases present in police violence, and helps in grasping the severity of the current situation.
