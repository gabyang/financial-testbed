HAMISH MCRAE: Make America great again? It already is Mr Trump and here's why...

By HAMISH MCRAE FOR THE DAILY MAIL

Updated: 22:03 GMT, 24 October 2016

  • e-mail

31

View
comments

Donald Trump wants to Make America Great Again. Well, here's a powerful reason to think it is great already.

The top nine of the largest companies in the world are all American, the top five all part of the high-tech revolution.

By contrast the most valuable European company, Royal Dutch Shell is number 18 and the largest purely British one, HSBC, is number 39.

This is utter domination. Europe is nowhere and even China is way behind, with Alibaba at number ten.

Bluff and bluster: Donald Trump wants to 'Make America great again'... while there's powerful reason to think it is already

But it is a domination that carries with it a variety of concerns. One is that US Inc is overly dependent on a single sector, high tech.

As it happens number one, Apple, and number two, Google (which now calls itself Alphabet) both report this week – Apple later today and Google on Thursday.

The Apple story will be mixed, and it is expected by the market that profits will have declined for three quarters on the trot.

It is a company that has to run very hard to maintain its position, and some day its great run will falter. It has radically scaled back its car project, suggesting a need to focus on the core products rather than the visionary ones.

Google by contrast had its shares yesterday trading at an all-time high and is expected to show a sharp rise in profits.

Apple and Google are followed in size by Microsoft, Amazon and Facebook. If you think about it, nearly all of us use their services all the time, and that points to a vulnerability for the rest of us. We can't do without them.

Microsoft has just announced it will increase the price of its business software and cloud services by up to 22 per cent following the fall of sterling.

Its consumer products won't be affected but if you are a business you probably have to stump up. You cannot, like Marmite, spread the stuff more thinly.

There's a third concern. These companies know so much about us. They know what we read, what we buy, where we are. They also shape things.

For example, nearly half of Americans rely on Facebook for their news and it claims to be a neutral platform. But inevitably it does make editorial judgements, based on what people have previously chosen to read.

Most of us are very happy to have all these services and in a way the United States has been extraordinarily open in the way it has allowed the rest of the world to have access to the genius of its high-tech companies.

Think what might have happened had Russia created a Facebook.

But of course it couldn't. Nor could we in Britain. Nor anyone in Europe. We can create content – for example, MailOnline is the world's largest newspaper website.

But we can't create high-tech giants that dominate global markets. America can, and that, Mr Trump, is why it remains great.

Carney's qualities

Mark Carney appears before the House of Lords today in what will probably be the Bank of England's governor's last public statement before the interest rate decision next week.

On Thursday we get the third quarter GDP figures, which look like confirming that there has been decent growth, running at somewhere between 0.3 per cent and 0.5 per cent. So no post-Brexit slump, at least not yet.

This will put pressure on Carney for crying wolf about the dangers of Brexit in the run-up to the referendum, and arguably for that cut in interest rates in August.

Did the economy need an artificial boost? Was it really a good idea, given that the cut helped trash sterling?

More generally, have the costs of ultra-cheap money, including the damage to savers and to pension funds, begun to outweigh the supposed benefits?

But while there are plenty of reasons to attack central bank policy, challengers should play the ball, not the man.

The Bank of England is independent for a reason, which is that politicians used to manipulate interest rates for perceived political advantage.

The Bank's monetary policy committee was established under statute and is responsible to Parliament.

There is a further reason for supporting Carney right now. His five-year term of office – shortened at his request from the usual eight years – ends in June 2018, in all probability in the middle of the Brexit negotiations.

He has promised to give early notice as to whether he would like it extended.

Whatever you think of his call on interest rates or his warning on Brexit there are three powerful reasons for the Government to urge him to stay.

One is the adverse signal it would give to global finance were he to leave. He carries a lot of respect from his days as head of the Bank of Canada, helping the country avoid the worst of the financial crisis.

The second is that he is not a grandstander but will focus on the detail of a successful negotiation, exactly what is needed.

And last, he is trusted – a quality not to be underrated in tricky times.
