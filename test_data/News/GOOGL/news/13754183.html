Does The Relaunch Of Google Meet Breach Antitrust Law?

Enrique Dans

As lockdown spread around the world from March onward, Zoom found itself in an enviable fast-growing situation: created by an Eric Yuan already with notable experience after participating in Cisco’s development of Webex as VP of Engineering, and positioned as the video conferencing application that best managed latency, it enjoyed enormous growth in all its audience segments, sending its share price up from $96.39 to $243 today, and making it one of the big winners of the pandemic.

In response to Zoom’s boom, Google reacted by taking a relatively minor product in its portfolio, Google Meet, which until now had been reserved exclusively for users of G Suite and G Suite for Education, and offering it openly and free of charge to all users. In a short time, downloads of the tool quickly rose to 50 million, which was logical in the context of a pandemic that had half the world confined to their homes. But in addition, Google completed the move with a product bundling strategy: it linked Google Meet to its email product,Gmail, which is widely used by more than 1.5 billion people.

In addition, Google has recently introduced a machine learning-based background noise cancellation system for Meet that can eliminate potentially annoying sounds during a video conference such as ringing, barking, keyboard noise, etc., which has received considerable attention.

On one side, we have Google, one of the largest technology companies in the world, with practically unlimited development capacity and a wide portfolio of products, some of which, such as Gmail, are used by many users around the world. On the other side, Zoom, a rising star, with undoubted potential and already in the public market, but much smaller and based, for the moment, on a single product. Without a doubt, an enormously unequal contest, qualified only by a fundamental issue for this type of product: for whatever reason, Zoom is able to manage latency during video conferences much better, which means that, with the same bandwidth and equipment, a video conference in Zoom looks better, sounds better, and is interrupted less often than in Google Meet (and much less than Skype, Teams, HouseParty or any of the other many systems I have tested).

So what are we talking about here? What happens when a company with a leading position in the market goes all out into competition with a smaller one, and does it by putting a free product on the market and grouping it with another in which it has a very important market share? This is essentially what led to Microsoft being hit in 2001 with an antitrust lawsuit. What was Microsoft doing? Whenever the success of another company’s product caught its attention, it bundled one with similar features to its ubiquitous Windows system, making it extremely difficult to compete with it.

I’ve discussed the events of 2001 and the trial that lasted several years, because I had the opportunity to study them in detail during my doctorate at UCLA. At that time, antitrust law was not being used much in the United States, and there were very few cases in which the company being sued failed to win. Now, in an environment of much greater control and regulation over large technology companies, the outcome could be different.

What are the consequences of doing nothing, and of allowing the large technology companies to compete without any restrictions with free or product bundling strategies such as those mentioned? Basically, that competing with them becomes practically impossible: they will always be in a position to acquire those companies with products that stand out, or if they cannot do so, to imitate their products until they manage to suffocate them, as we have seen on numerous occasions. In the long term, the consequences for innovation are obvious.

Did Google violate anti-monopoly laws by relaunching Google Meet for free and linking it to Gmail to try to compete with Zoom? If it’s not a textbook case, it certainly looks like one.
