From ‘BIC For Her’ To BIC For Them - How Cancel Culture Got BIC Back On Track

2012 wasn’t a great year for BIC. Despite fifty-plus years of global success (with the 100 billionth sold in 2006, the BIC ‘Cristal’ is the best-selling pen in the world), one product launch got the brand canceled overnight.

I am, of course, talking about BIC For Her—the pink and purple pens marketed to women as an “accessory” (just for us girls!) designed to fit comfortably in our apparently-abnormal hands.

The sexism-tainted launch sparked widespread outrage and ridicule, resulting in global news coverage, thought pieces, and an Amazon page flooded with fake reviews.

One BIC For Her ‘customer’ wrote: “Before these pens, I was nothing. I was a mere inconsequential woman, stumbling around writing nonsense with big pens that made me look ridiculous.

“I could barely write my name without having to sit down afterwards- it's nine letters long and thus really takes it out of me when writing with a normal pen designed for men.

“But now...the whole world looks different. I cannot recommend this pen enough. It won't just change your handwriting. It will change your life.”

The associated tags—which Amazon uses to guide customers as they shop—included ‘soft-brained woman’, ‘delicate flowers’, ‘small lady hands’ and ‘sexist’.

Understandably, BIC discontinued its gender-blighted pens and went back to the proverbial drawing board.

Flash forward eight years, to present day, where BIC finally seems back on track. Thanks, in large part, to its ultra-dynamic North America General Manager, Mary Fox.

Since joining the company less than two years ago, Fox has worked fast and furious to right the wrongs of the company’s prior gaffe. First, with a premium razor targeted at men and women.

"We started working initially on Made For You, which we launched this time last year with Amazon,” she says. “It was a great partner to do this with because you can learn, real-time, how consumers feel.”

There is truly no better way to describe Fox than consumer-obsessed. The British-born businesswoman moved to the US with Walmart in 2005 to handle mergers and acquisitions (M&A) strategy, and moved up the ranks quickly, but found "all roads lead to Bentonville [Arkansas, home to Walmart HQ].

“I was having my first baby, living in New York, loving Walmart, but knew I needed to stay here with my husband and baby.”

Facing an all-too-common dilemma for working women, Fox took a job at L’Oreal so she could continue caring for and living with her family.

"I really enjoyed being on the other side of brands—really understanding the importance of connection, bringing something to life and connecting it with what consumers need or want in their life,” she says. “Beauty, particularly, is a bit of both."

Admitting her time at L'Oreal felt "very bureaucratic—I struggled to get as much done", a chance meeting with the head of BIC led to a new job offer with more autonomy and opportunity.

"We've been working on significantly transforming the business and being a lot more consumer-centric. Asking 'how do we go to market?' and 'how are we incredibly relevant today, tomorrow, and so forth?'”

Her day is now split into thirds; a third on customer growth, a third on customer behaviour ("how they're buying things last week, this week, and how do we anticipate what's on the horizon"), and a third on stakeholders, whether that's retail partners or internal teams.

"When I first started, one of the key things, as well as learning the business, was really listening to consumers. I did that across all of the categories, but more specifically with shavers.

“I was sitting in on shopper panels, dealing with lots of consumer insight, and what became very clear was that the shopping aisle was very gendered - very male specific, very female specific - and younger consumers, 18 to 34, said they just wanted a really great shave. They didn't need it to be male or female, but they wanted a unisex product."

Though it would have been easiest to repackage an existing shaver in a gender-neutral shell and call it a win, Fox took the hard road, urging the team to develop a brand new product that would serve the needs of men, women and everyone in between.

"If you say 'what do you need functionally?' and the answer is a great shave, you have to give the consumer a great shave.”

BIC-commissioned research found that 69% of consumers 18-34 years old believe companies should create more unisex personal care and beauty products and 75% of consumers under 34 are interested in using a razor that is marketed toward both women and men.

“We learned younger consumers just wanted to have a shave and not be defined but, sometimes, the gendered element is more important than the product itself.”

As such, BIC still sells gendered products, as well as its unisex Made For Yourazor and Us.—a brand new gender-neutral grooming line featuring razors, shave cream, body lotion and face lotion.

“It's not for everybody. We'll always have gendered products because I think that's a very important cue for people around how they shop, but I do think we will see the middle of the aisle—where products can for anybody and everybody—open up.”

Under Fox’s care it seems BIC has not only become obsessed with, but attentive to, the needs of its consumer.

"I mean, it was funny, when I joined I heard quite a bit about BIC For Her and, for me, the key piece was that we always need to be very customer-centric.

“There were trends that were identified when it was first launched, but I think it's so important that you're always thinking about how consumers want to be sold to, marketed to, and talked to. I think the lesson learned in anything, not just BIC For Her, is that if something hasn't worked, invariably, we didn't really listen to the consumers enough or really understand what they meant.”

With Fox’s encouragement, BIC now brings its consumer closer in, from research and development processes all the way through to marketing.

“Us. has been an amazing journey. We started with a consumer insight that was so strong and became louder louder, the more we actually really listened to it.”

In its 2020 Global Annual Review, market research firm Mintel INTC confirmed that consumers were ‘embracing gender neutrality,’ noting that 69% of consumers aged 18-22 have used or are interested in using genderless beauty products.

“We really worked very closely with the R&D team to see and understand how we could develop a product that fulfils on the promise and commitment that people were looking for.

“The time to develop the product was a lot faster [than normal], because we worked together, consumer-centricity working with R&D. Before, it was kind of R&D would do everything and then pass it on to the teams.”

While such products would typically takes two full years to develop, Fox has developed and launched Made For You, Us., and BodyMark—a small range of body-safe temporary tattoo pens—in just over one year.

“We were working very agilely—'round the kitchen table, we call it—to really listen and then make decisions. It really changed the dynamic of how we work and, frankly, set the bar for how we should work going forward.”
