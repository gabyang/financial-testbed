The best charcoal grills offer a flavorful, fun and versatile way to cook meat, seafood and vegetables outdoors. After testing six grills by cooking kielbasa sausages, chicken, fish, burgers, steaks, eggplant, cabbage and more, the Napoleon 22-Inch Charcoal Kettle Grill won as the best charcoal grill overall because of its cooking power, sturdy build and easy-to-assemble design. The classic Weber 22-Inch Original Kettle Premium Charcoal Grill was a close second. It’s more compact, but sits slightly lower down, which may make it harder to use for some people. For the best cart-style charcoal grill, the Dyna Glo Heavy-Duty Charcoal Grill is a standout choice if you want extra space to store your ingredients. If you want a more versatile grill, we also liked the Slow 'N Sear Original Kettle Grill, which comes with an insert to help with smoking or low and slow cooking.

Here are the best grills based on our testing:

  • Best Charcoal Grill Overall:Napoleon 22-Inch Charcoal Kettle Grill
  • Best Classic Charcoal Grill:Weber 22-Inch Original Kettle Premium Charcoal Grill
  • Best Cart-Style Charcoal Grill:Dyna-Glo Heavy-Duty Charcoal Grill
  • Best Charcoal Grill And Smoker:Slow 'N Sear Charcoal Kettle Grill And Smoker

The best charcoal grills can get much hotter than a stove or even a gas grill, imparting a smoky flavor and char that makes for mouth-watering meals. They involve more prep and cleanup than their gas counterparts, but many people think it’s worth the extra work—and even enjoy it. “If you like the journey or the process, then charcoal is the way to go,” said Steven Raichlen, a television host and author of a dozen books on grilling and barbecuing. Plus, there’s the appeal of cooking outside: grilling is a great way to host parties or simply enjoy your yard in the warmer months.

When choosing the best charcoal grill for you, you can consider budget, cooking surface area and extra features like storage or smoking capabilities. Kia Damon, a chef, writer, host and recipe tester and developer, tested six grills to help us find the best. We also consulted Raichlen and Max Good, vice president of product reviews at AmazingRibs.com, for additional guidance. Here are the best charcoal grills based on our testing.

Best Charcoal Grill Overall

A Taller Grill So You Won’t Have To Stoop While You Cook

Napoleon 22-Inch Charcoal Kettle Grill

Style: Kettle | Warranty: 10 years | Grill plates: Coated stainless steel

Best for:

  • Beginner cooks
  • Those who want a taller kettle grill

Skip if:

  • You want extra side table storage for your tools and ingredients

While you can find many versions of a classic kettle-shaped charcoal grill, the Napoleon 22-Inch Charcoal Kettle Grill stood out as the best charcoal grill in our testing thanks to its superior design and cooking prowess. Most notably, the Napoleon stands over 10 inches taller than the Weber 22-Inch Original Kettle Premium Charcoal Grill, which was a close runner-up. The height difference was noticeable while cooking, and you might appreciate that you don’t have to bend over or crouch down to tend the Napoleon, so you can grill more comfortably for longer periods of time. That’s especially helpful if you’re cooking up a feast for a crowd.

Compared to the competition, the Napoleon also stayed hotter and cooked longer with the two chimneys of charcoal used in testing, acing a range of cooking tasks from searing thick steaks to roasting more delicate eggplant. With just one and a half chimneys of charcoal, the grill quickly reached a high searing temperature, allowing us to cook for hours without needing to replenish the charcoal. Meat cooked on this grill, including steaks, sausages, burgers and a whole chicken, had even sear marks and great browning. The grill was also big enough to set up two-zone cooking to grill foods like fish or cabbage that needed gentler heat.

Several other design features helped the Napoleon shine as the best charcoal grill in testing. It has four legs to the Weber’s three, which helped it feel more stable during outdoor cooking. While it doesn’t have a side table like a cart-style grill, the Napoleon has a rectangular storage shelf below the grill that’s roomier than the Weber’s triangular, more awkwardly-shaped storage shelf. The shelf came in handy while testing to stash sheet pans, bowls, utensils and even charcoal for easy access. The Napoleon has a hook to hang the lid on the side of the grill as well. Setup and cleanup with the Napoleon was also a breeze—even with just one person.

The Napoleon comes with staple features included in the best charcoal grills. You can lift the hinged grate to add more charcoal as needed, adjust the vents to control cooking temperature and utilize the ash catcher to easily empty the grill for cleaning.

The only downside to the Napoleon is that the coated grates may peel over time (the Weber’s grates are made from the same material). For advanced grillers, Raichlen recommends cast iron grates, which will retain heat more effectively and last longer. You can also buy a cast iron grate for the Napoleon. The Napoleon also comes with a generous 10-year warranty should any issues arise.

Best Classic Charcoal Grill

Style: Kettle | Warranty: 10 years | Grill plates: Coated stainless steel

Best for:

  • Brand loyalists

The Weber 22-Inch Original Kettle Premium Charcoal Grill grill has a sleek, unfussy design that makes it easy to assemble and grill on. Cooking-wise, this grill performed on a par with the Napoleon in testing. The Weber Premium yielded smoky, juicy meats from chicken to steak, and perfectly charred vegetables. If you have limited storage or yard space, this grill has a slightly smaller footprint than the Napoleon, but it is shorter, so you may have to bend down more while cooking.

Heat stayed consistent on the Weber Premium during testing, fluctuating between 450 and 500 degrees. Meats cooked evenly with excellent browning and searing, and the grill is big enough to set up two-zone cooking. Unlike the Napoleon, however, the Weber Premium needed additional charcoal halfway through the testing process.

One standout feature on the Weber Premium is the One-Touch Ash Removal: simply switch a lever, and a fan-shaped mechanism pushes ashes from the grill into the ash catcher below to make for easier cleaning. The Weber Premium was similarly easy to clean as the Napoleon in our testing. It comes with a 10-year warranty, too. Overall, the Weber Premium is one of the best charcoal grills we tested; if the height of your grill doesn’t matter as much to you, it’s an excellent choice.

If you want a bigger cooking surface and extra storage space on your grill, or you’re a more advanced griller, a cart-style grill like the Dyna-Glo Heavy-Duty Charcoal Grill is your best option. The Dyna-Glo had several features that made for great grilling, especially if you want to fine-tune your cooking temperatures. An adjustable charcoal rack helps you control cooking temperatures by bringing the charcoal closer, or farther away, from your food. This grill has a cast-iron front access door that lets you easily add charcoal or stoke your coals, helpful if you’re grilling over a longer period of time or want to move coals to one area to set up two-zone grilling. The Dyna-Glo also has a flue to help adjust temperature, which can come in handy for grilling more delicate foods like vegetables or fish. These extra features are great for grilling meals involving different types of foods, like meats, fish and vegetables. Beginner grillers may prefer a more basic grill, though.

Compared to the kettle grills on this list, the Dyna-Glo also has a larger cooking surface (it measures 816 square inches, which translates to holding about 36 burgers) and more storage options. Two side tables pop out to hold utensils, seasoning or your prepped ingredients; a roomy wire storage compartment at the bottom of the grill can hold used pans, bowls or whatever else you need it to.

A larger cart-style charcoal grill like the Dyna-Glo is more difficult to set up and maintain compared to the compact, basic kettle grills on this list. The tradeoff may be worth it for a larger, more feature-packed grill—and you can always recruit some help for assembly and cooking cleanup.

How We Tested The Best Charcoal Grills

To find the best grills to test, we spent weeks poring over editorial guides and product reviews. Kia Damon, a chef, writer, host, and recipe tester and developer, tested six grills for this piece, and interviewed Max Good ofAmazingRibs.com for insights on what makes the best charcoal grill. Additional reporting was provided by Forbes Vetted senior reviews editor Anna Perling, who interviewed Steven Raichlen, a television host and author of a dozen books on grilling and barbecuing including The Barbecue Bible.

When testing charcoal grills, Damon considered ease of use and design, cooking performance and ease of cleaning. She built all of the grills to test for ease of assembly, considering clarity of instructions and whether or not a grill could be set up by one person.

After starting the grills with two chimneys of charcoal, Damon grilled a range of foods to check for even cooking and heat retention, including burgers, steaks, chicken, fish, kielbasa sausage, vegetables and more. On the burgers and sausages, she looked for browning on the outside, and even cooking and juiciness inside. She tested steak and chicken to see how well grills could brown larger cuts of meat. For vegetables like eggplant and Napa cabbage, and fish, she tested whether grills could handle more delicate foods on lower heat.

Lastly, Damon cleaned the grills, considering how easy it was to remove grates and ash, and whether a grill had additional features to make cleaning easier.

How To Pick A Charcoal Grill

Picking a charcoal can feel like a daunting task—there are various styles, shapes and features to choose from. Here’s what to consider.

Capacity

Consider how large you want your cooking area to be, and how many people you’ll usually cook for. A compact kettle grill will be great for whipping up grilled chicken and vegetables on summer nights for dinner. Good advised choosing at least a 22-inch sized kettle grill to be big enough for two-zone cooking, which allows you to cook over direct and indirect heat. A roomier cart-style grill will have a bigger cooking surface, and may be better for entertaining a crowd and cranking out dozens of burgers.

Design

Different grills will have different designs for controlling air flow (and thus temperature), adding charcoal and storing cooking tools. Consider your cooking needs and what will be easier for you to use. For example, consider whether you’d like to adjust a damper or a flue to control air flow, and if you’d prefer to lift a hinged grate to add charcoal, or to open a grill’s front door. Storage-wise, you might opt for special features like side tables, storage shelves or hooks to hang lids.

Maintenance

All charcoal grills will involve maintenance, primarily removing ashes and cleaning the grill. Some grills have coated grates that may make it easier to clean, as well as ash-removal designs that make it simpler to dump out ashes after cooking.

What’s The Difference Between A Gas And Charcoal Grill?

The main difference between a gas and charcoal grill lies in how hot each can get. “Charcoal grills have a much wider temperature range than most gas grills,” says Good. Hotter cooking temperatures allow for more effective searing, and lower temperatures will be best for slow cooking or smoking. You can also move your charcoal around to create two-zone cooking, with hotter and cooler areas, to cook different foods. Cooking over charcoal also imparts a characteristic smoky flavor to foods like burgers, meats and vegetables. Typically, charcoal grills are less expensive and easier to set up compared to gas grills since you won’t need to hook up a charcoal grill to a fuel source like a propane tank. While all grills will require cleaning, you will have to remove the ash from a charcoal grill.

How Do You Clean A Charcoal Grill?

All grills will require maintenance. You’ll need to remove the ash from a charcoal grill, but you should also maintain the grates and outside of the grill. “Always clean the grates. It's a good idea to have a good grill grate brush,” says Good. To remove food debris, start cleaning while the grates are still hot. “Brush it [the grates] with a stiff wire brush or a wooden scraper,” says Raichlen. Once your grill cools completely, carefully remove the charcoal ashes. Don’t spray the ashes with water—the combination of ashes and water can corrode the grill. To clean the lid, scrub off any grime or buildup using a sponge or rag and hot soapy water.

What Is The Difference Between A Kettle Grill And A Charcoal Grill?

Charcoal grills come in various shapes and sizes. A kettle grill is a specific type of charcoal grill. Kettle grills are named for their distinctive round pot-shaped body and lid. Our top pick, the Napoleon 22-Inch Charcoal Kettle Grill, is a kettle-style grill. These grills are most well-known for their affordability and versatility, making kettle grills an excellent choice for any griller.

How Do You Light A Charcoal Grill?

To light most charcoal grills, you will need to use a chimney starter. “[P]ut the coals in the top, [add] either crumpled newspaper or paraffin fire starter in the bottom, and within 15 to 20 minutes you have perfectly and evenly lit charcoal,” says Raichlen. Depending on the size of your grill, you may need to use more than 1 chimney starter simultaneously.
